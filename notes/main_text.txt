DEFINING COMPOSITIONAL REPRESENTATIONS
BY RECOVERABILITY
Karthik Singaravadivelan
College of Computing, Georgia Institute of Technology
October 27, 2026
ABSTRACT
People understand a sentence they have never heard because they can build its meaning from parts they already know,
and they can take the meaning apart again into the same parts. Machine learning now calls almost any success-
ful recombination of familiar material compositional, and recent work has rightly separated behavioral evidence of
compositionality from representational evidence. Yet what a representation must be like to count as compositional re-
mains unsettled. Here, we formalize a simple idea: a representation is compositional when it combines parts through
a relation from which every part, including a part the procedure was never ﬁtted on, can be read back in its role by
the same ﬁxed procedure of a declared kind, such as an afﬁne map, and into which each part enters every role through
one shared code. We show that recovery must be measured over the distribution of parts, on parts not used to ﬁt the
readout. On any ﬁnite vocabulary, almost every way of combining parts can be decoded by lookup, so recovery of
familiar parts cannot separate composition from blending; recovery of freshly drawn parts can, its ceiling is set by
the rank of a linear relation and, for afﬁne readouts, by the width of any state, and a shared part code ﬁxes every role
reversal from a single composite. We then test the deﬁnition. Analytic modes of combination match the predicted
ceilings, and ﬁxed-width vector-symbolic superposition of dense parts behaves, under afﬁne readouts, like a lossy
blend. In small networks trained on glyph composites, the states keep fresh glyphs recoverable at every vocabulary
size; the networks’ own decoders read them only after training on many distinct glyphs, like readouts ﬁtted on the
training glyphs alone; and the role components we recover are the ones the decoders use. The learned recursive
composer shows the same split one level beyond its training depths. Pretrained text encoders recover nouns on which
the readout was not ﬁtted only partly; GPT-2 comes closest to a role–ﬁller reference, and CLIP’s text encoders do no
better than the same architecture with random weights. Finally, we discuss what a network must do to earn the term:
allocate room for every part, learn its own procedures from many distinct parts, share one part code across roles, use
the role components it represents, and keep its relation invertible on its own outputs.
Keywords compositionality, systematicity, representation, binding, neural networks
1
Introduction
What we mean when we say that linguistic capacities are systematic is that the
ability to produce/understand some sentences is intrinsically connected to the
ability to produce/understand certain others.
Fodor and Pylyshyn (1988)
Compositionality is among the oldest ideas in the study of mind and language. Frege (1923) observed that we under-
stand sentences we have never heard because we can build the sense of a sentence from parts that correspond to its
words, and Fodor and Pylyshyn (1988) argued that the systematicity and productivity of human thought are explained
by representations with constituent structure. In machine learning the idea appears as compositional generalization,
the ability to produce correct outputs for novel combinations of familiar atoms (Lake and Baroni, 2018; Keysers et al.,
2020; Kim and Linzen, 2020). Text-to-image models are described as composing concepts into new scenes (Ramesh
et al., 2021; Okawa et al., 2023), and language models as reasoning compositionally (Press et al., 2023); yet the same
models often fail to place objects in requested relations (Conwell and Ullman, 2022) and express an attribute named
for one object on another (Rassin et al., 2022).

Most uses of the term rest on a colloquial principle that descends from Frege and Montague (1970): the meaning
or representation of a complex is determined by those of its parts and the way they are combined. Any system
that computes a function of its inputs satisﬁes it (Zadrozny, 1994; Westerståhl, 1998), so it cannot distinguish, in
representational form, an avocado placed on a chair from an armchair in the shape of an avocado (Figure 1). Vegner
et al. (2025) distinguished behavioral from representational systematicity, the ﬁrst a property of outputs on unseen
combinations and the second of the internal states, and argued that benchmarks of the ﬁrst do not establish the second.
Existing proposals measure the simplicity of a symbolic redescription (Elmoznino et al., 2025), the novel structure
in the best model of a whole beyond the best models of its parts (Elmoznino et al., 2026), or how well an explicit
composition of inferred primitives reproduces a state (Andreas, 2019). A separate line of work proves that additive,
invertible decoders generalize to unseen combinations of slots (Lachapelle et al., 2023; Wiedemer et al., 2024), and that
linear, orthogonal factors are necessary for compositional generalization in embedding models (Uselis et al., 2026).
What none of these settles is when recovering parts from a representation is evidence of compositional structure, and
when it is evidence only of a familiar vocabulary.
Our starting point is that a compositional representation must let its parts be read back, in their roles, by a ﬁxed
procedure that does not depend on which parts they are. It is close to the reverse compositionality that Fodor and
Lepore (2002) defended and Robbins (2005) criticized, the claim that understanding a complex expression includes
understanding its constituents. It also bears on van Gelder (1990)’s distinction: a representation can be functionally
compositional, with parts that are recoverable, without being concatenative, with parts that are tokened. Section 2
develops this idea and shows, with one running example, why the decisive test is recovery of parts on which the
reading procedure was never ﬁtted.
Section 3 formalizes the deﬁnition. A relation is recoverable when readouts from a declared class return each part in
its role across the whole distribution of parts, which we estimate on parts that played no role in ﬁtting the readouts;
it is role-factorized when each part enters every role through one shared code. We prove that unrestricted versions of
both properties are vacuous, give ceilings on recovery for linear relations and, under afﬁne readouts, for arbitrary ones,
and show that under a role-factorized relation every role reversal and every new assignment of a known part to a role
is ﬁxed by a single composite.
We test the deﬁnition in Section 4 on analytic modes of combination, on networks trained from scratch, including
interventions on their states, on recursive composition, and on eleven pretrained text encoders. We close in Section 5 by
stating what a network must do to count as compositional, and by discussing implications for terminology, architecture,
and evaluation.
2
Intuition for recoverable composition
Consider two ways of combining an avocado and a chair (Figure 1). In the ﬁrst, the avocado ﬁlls a role of the relation
on: the scene contains both objects intact, and an observer can say which object rests on which. In the second, the
features of the two concepts merge into an avocado-shaped armchair, and neither concept survives as a part. Both are
functions of the two parts, and both can be produced for a pair of parts never seen together, so the colloquial principle
and behavioral tests treat them alike.
The difference lies in what can be read back. Suppose a system represents composites as vectors and combines two
part vectors x and y by adding them. Every pair on the line x + y = z yields the same composite z (Figure 7a), so
no procedure, however clever, can return the parts. A tensor product binding, x ⊗ρ1 + y ⊗ρ2 with independent role
vectors, keeps one decomposition for each composite (Figure 7b). Three cases illustrate how this difference appears
in practice.
A small vocabulary hides the difference. If the system only ever combines parts from a vocabulary of, say, seven
known vectors, few vocabulary pairs lie on any one line x+y = z, and a lookup over the vocabulary recovers the parts
of almost every sum. The sum then looks recoverable. The recoverability comes from the sparsity of the vocabulary,
not from the relation, and it disappears for a part outside the vocabulary.
Fresh parts expose it. If we draw a new pair of parts and add them, the composite lies on a line that contains inﬁnitely
many other decompositions, and nothing distinguishes the true one. Under the tensor product, the same ﬁxed readout
that worked for familiar parts returns the new ones.
A shared code ﬁxes reversals. If each part enters each role through one code, transformed only by the role, then reading
a composite once determines its role reversal and where a part seen only in one role would go in the other. Nothing

For an arbitrary system, then, the question is whether some ﬁxed readout, of a kind declared in advance, recovers each

3
Deﬁning representational compositionality
3.1
Preliminaries
A part is drawn from a distribution D over a space of possible parts, which may be continuous (glyphs, vectors, images)
or very large (the nouns of a language). A system assigns each part x a part code e(x) ∈Rd, its representation of
the part on its own, and assigns a composite r(x1, . . . , xk) of k parts in roles 1, . . . , k of a relation r an internal state
h = gr(x1, . . . , xk) ∈Rm. Training exposes the system to a ﬁnite vocabulary V of parts drawn from D; a fresh part
is a further draw from D outside V. Freshness is always relative to a procedure: a part is fresh to a procedure if no
composite containing it was used to ﬁt that procedure, even if the part itself is known. Deﬁnition 1 needs only parts
fresh to the readout; whether a system’s own decoding procedure handles parts fresh to it is a separate, behavioral
question (Table 1). For glyphs and objects, a part can be fresh to both. For a word in a pretrained model it can be
fresh only to the readout. For a concept in a person, the relevant sense is fresh to the person’s composition procedure:
a word or object learned in isolation, or in one role, and never yet combined, since a concept the person does not
possess cannot be a constituent of a thought they entertain (Fodor and Lepore, 2002). What D is for concepts is an
idealization; for words it can be operationalized by nonce forms with taught meanings. Composites can serve as parts,
so the setup is closed under combination. A readout class U, such as afﬁne maps, is the family of procedures we allow
for recovering a part’s code from a composite state; a readout ui ∈U is ﬁtted for each role i and then held ﬁxed.
Two observations, proved in Appendix A, show why the class of readouts and the use of fresh parts cannot be left
open. First, if the coordinates in which a relation is required to be additive are unrestricted, every invertible relation
is additive in some coordinates, so additivity adds nothing to invertibility (Lemma 1). Second, on a ﬁnite vocabulary
every relation that is injective on the vocabulary is inverted exactly by a lookup table, so recovery of familiar parts
cannot separate composition from blending (Lemma 2). Both properties have content only when the readouts and
coordinates are restricted in advance and recovery is measured on parts that played no role in ﬁtting them.

This criterion asks two things of a relation. Recoverability, Eq. (1), asks that a ﬁxed procedure of a declared kind return
each part in its role across the whole part distribution, not only for the parts from which the procedure was learned;
1 −ε is the population R2 of the best readout in U. The deﬁnition does not depend on how many parts a system or
an experimenter happens to have seen. Its estimation does: a readout ﬁtted on composites of a small vocabulary is
undetermined outside the span of that vocabulary (Proposition 5), and scoring it on the same vocabulary inﬂates it
(Lemma 2). Both biases vanish when the readout is ﬁtted on a large probe pool and evaluated on fresh parts, which
is why fresh parts are the unit of evidence. Role-factorization, Eq. (2), asks that each part contribute the same code
to every whole, transformed only by its role, which is the classical requirement that a constituent contribute the same
thing to every complex in which it occurs (Fodor and Pylyshyn, 1988), with the coordinates restricted to afﬁne maps
so that the requirement has content. Intuitively, a relation fails the ﬁrst criterion when it discards information about its
parts, as, under afﬁne readouts, every blend into a space smaller than its parts must (Proposition 4), and fails the second
when what a part contributes depends on its co-parts, as in prototype combination (Smith et al., 1988; Hampton, 1987).
Deﬁnition 2. Representational compositionality. A system is representationally compositional with respect to
a relation r, relative to a declared readout class U and a declared class M of methods for ﬁnding part codes,
to the degree that, for some part code e found under M that is informative about the parts, its realization of r
is ε-recoverable and δ-role-factorized. We report the degree by δ and by the normalized score κ = (εblend −
ε)/(εblend −εref), where εref and εblend are obtained by the same procedure from a role–ﬁller operator and a
symmetric blend applied to the same part codes; the system is compositional at tolerance τ if κ ≥1 −τ and
δ ≤τ. A part code is informative if parts can be identiﬁed from it among held-out candidates, or if it retains a
declared share of the variance of a description of the parts that does not depend on the system. The relation is
productive to depth n if the same holds when the parts are themselves composites of depth up to n −1, with the
state of each composite as its part code.
Note that Deﬁnition 2 is graded and relative. It is graded because ε and δ are continuous, and we calibrate them against
reference operators built from the system’s own part codes, since the best attainable values depend on the parts and
on the readout class (Section 4.5). It is relative to U and M because the decomposition must be found by search;
a negative result is therefore reported as the absence of a decomposition under declared methods, and the methods
must be validated on systems whose decomposition is known. Restricting U is not a convenience: with unrestricted
readouts, as with unrestricted causal alignment maps (Sutter et al., 2025) and unrestricted probes (Hewitt and Liang,
2019), any injective system passes. Requiring the part code to be informative closes a second loophole: the blend
g(x, y) = (Px, Py) with P a projection of rank r < d discards d −r dimensions of each part, yet with the code
e(x) = Px it is exactly recoverable and role-factorized. A verdict about blending is therefore always relative to a
declared part code, and we report how much of a system-independent description of the parts each code retains.

3.3
What the deﬁnition accounts for
Appendix A proves two lemmas and ten propositions; we state the four results that carry the argument.
Blends cannot be read back, and how much they lose is computable. If a relation is linear, g(x1, . . . , xk) = Ax
with A = [A1 · · · Ak], and its image is smaller than the space of its parts, each composite has an afﬁne family of
decompositions of dimension kd −rank A (Proposition 2). For Gaussian parts the loss is exact: no readout of any
kind recovers role i with R2 above tr(A⊤
i (AA⊤)+Ai)/d, and the average over roles is rank A/(kd) (Proposition 3).
The ceiling is 0.8 for role 1 of x + 0.5y, 0.5 for either role of x + y, and m/(kd) for a generic projection into m < kd
dimensions. Intuitively, a blend keeps only as many dimensions of its parts as its image has room for.
For trained systems, whose relations are nonlinear, a weaker but general bound applies: whatever the relation and
whatever the distribution of parts, an afﬁne readout of an m-dimensional state explains at most the share of the parts’
variance carried by the top m principal components of the stacked part codes, which is m/(kd) for isotropic codes
(Proposition 4).
Familiar parts can be decoded anyway. When parts come from a ﬁnite vocabulary, a non-invertible relation can be
injective on that vocabulary, and its parts can be decoded by clean-up (Plate, 1995, 2003; Frady et al., 2018). This
recoverability comes from the sparsity of the vocabulary rather than from the relation, and it fails for parts outside
the vocabulary. The same holds for any procedure learned from a vocabulary, whether an experimenter’s readout
or a network’s own decoder: an afﬁne procedure ﬁtted on composites is determined only on the span of the ﬁtted
states, which covers fresh composites only if the codes of the parts in each role span Rd (Proposition 5). A procedure
therefore has to be learned from enough distinct parts before it can handle new ones.

Role-factorization ﬁxes reversals and new role assignments from single composites. If a relation is role-factorized
with δ = 0 and the stacked matrix [B1 · · · Bk] has full column rank, then every role reversal and every assignment of
a known part to a role it has never occupied is an afﬁne function of a single composite containing it (Proposition 7).
This is the unbinding argument of tensor-product representations (Smolensky, 1990), and it is a statement about what
the states determine, not about what a system does with them. Fodor and McLaughlin (1990) objected to exactly
this defence that constituents which are merely recoverable need not be causally efﬁcacious in processing. Read in
interventionist terms, as a claim that the components need not make a difference to processing, the objection becomes
testable for a given system: an interchange intervention that replaces one role’s component of a state should change
the system’s behavior for that role alone (Section 4.3). Without role-factorization, observed composites constrain
unobserved ones only when the set of observed pairs connects every part to every role (Proposition 6), so behavior
on a new role assignment depends on the training data and is in this sense contingent (Aizawa, 2003). Whether a
learner acquires a role-factorized relation in the ﬁrst place remains contingent on its training, which is the part of the
systematicity challenge that a criterion on states cannot remove.
Productivity requires invertibility on the relation’s own outputs. A nested linear blend of depth n loses at least
(2n −1)d dimensions of its leaves (Proposition 9). A relation that is invertible on composites of parts need not be
invertible on composites of composites, which are drawn from a different distribution; productivity therefore requires
that the relation remain recoverable when applied to its own outputs, a property we test directly.
3.4
Relationship to other accounts
Identiﬁability of additive decoders. Our two conditions are not new as mathematics. Additive decoders, which
render each slot separately and sum the results, together with an encoder that inverts the decoder on recombined slots,
are the sufﬁcient conditions under which object-centric models provably generalize to unseen combinations of slots
(Brady et al., 2023; Lachapelle et al., 2023; Wiedemer et al., 2024), and Wiedemer et al. (2023) locate compositional
generalization in the data-generating process rather than the data. Our role-factorization is their additivity with afﬁne
coordinates, and our recoverability is a readout-side, afﬁne analogue of their consistency condition, which requires
an encoder to invert the decoder on recombined latents, and their caveat that additivity fails under occlusion is our
prediction for overlapping roles (Section 4.2). Two things are new. We use the conditions to deﬁne a representational
property and not as sufﬁcient conditions for a behavioral one, and we separate recovery by a population readout from
recovery by procedures learned on the system’s own vocabulary, which separates what the relation guarantees from
what a vocabulary supplies; in trained networks the two dissociate (Section 4.2). Fresh draws from D lie inside each
role’s support, so our criterion is an afﬁne, readout-side analogue of their target, not an extrapolation beyond it.
Necessary structure. Uselis et al. (2026) argue that compositional generalization in embedding models requires
representations that decompose linearly into per-concept components, orthogonal across concepts. Their result runs
from behavior to structure; Proposition 7 runs from structure to what the states determine. Our condition is weaker than
theirs: role-factorization asks that [B1 · · · Bk] have full column rank, not that the role subspaces be orthogonal, and a
full-rank but ill-conditioned B is recoverable with ampliﬁed noise. Schug et al. (2024) show that a number of training
tasks linear in the number of modules sufﬁces when the training support is connected, which is the learning-theoretic
analogue of Proposition 6, and Lippl and Stachenfeld (2025) show that kernel models generalize compositionally only
through conjunction-wise additive computations, with a “memorization leak” that corresponds to our residual η.
Decodability and generalization. Measuring whether a readout trained on some conditions transfers to held-out
conditions is standard in neuroscience as cross-condition generalization (Bernardi et al., 2020; Ito et al., 2022), and
linear decodability of constituents accompanies compositional generalization as models scale (Redhardt et al., 2025).
Decodable, disentangled factors do not by themselves ensure that a decoder generalizes (Montero et al., 2021), and
models can generalize without compositional messages (Chaabouni et al., 2020); these are the two off-diagonal cells
of Table 1. Discovered tensor-product decompositions of trained networks (McCoy et al., 2019; Soulos et al., 2020),
binding identiﬁers in language models (Feng and Steinhardt, 2024), and interchange interventions (Geiger et al., 2021,
2024) are methods that M can contain. What we add is the requirement that the readouts be evaluated on parts outside
the vocabulary from which they and the system were ﬁtted.

neither requires that parts be read back from the whole; a ﬁxed-width superposition over a small vocabulary compresses

The classical debate. Fodor and Pylyshyn (1988) argued that systematicity requires constituent structure, and Fodor
and McLaughlin (1990) that classical constituents are tokened whenever the complex is; they rejected Smolensky’s
view (Smolensky, 1987, 1988, 1990) that tensor-product constituents, recoverable but not tokened, sufﬁce, arguing
that such constituents are not causally efﬁcacious in processing. Hadley (1994) sharpened the behavioral side into
weak and strong systematicity, and Hupkes et al. (2020) and Vegner et al. (2025) turned it into tests for networks. Our
criterion takes Smolensky’s side on what a representation must contain and recasts Fodor and McLaughlin’s causal-
efﬁcacy objection in interventionist terms, which interchange interventions can test for a given system (Geiger et al.,
2021); their stronger demands, that constituents be tokened and that their efﬁcacy follow from the architecture, are
not addressed by any criterion on states. Goodale and Mascarenhas (2026) restate the challenge as one about what
an architecture guarantees by default, and Woydt et al. (2025) argue that meta-learning (Lake and Baroni, 2023) has
not yet produced human-like systematicity. Our criterion is a property of states, not of architectures, so a network can
meet it by training; Section 5 returns to this point.
4
Testing recoverability
The deﬁnition is useful only if it separates systems that differ in the way it claims. We ask ﬁve questions: do analytic
modes of combination meet the predicted ceilings? do the states of networks trained from scratch keep fresh parts
recoverable, and does the network itself use them? do the structural consequences of role-factorization hold in trained
networks, and are the role components causally used? does recovery survive recursion? and do pretrained encoders
recover nouns on which the readout was not ﬁtted? Each experiment below is fully characterized by:
• Composites: the parts, their distribution D, and the relation;
• Readouts: the declared class U, the parts on which it is ﬁtted, and the parts on which it is evaluated, which are
always disjoint from the former;
• Measures: recovery (R2 of the part code or identiﬁcation among candidates) and, for systems that act, behavior
on held-out pairs of training parts, on training parts in roles they never occupied, and on fresh parts.
Methods are summarized below; Appendix D gives full details, and every number is a mean over seeds with standard
errors in the ﬁgures.

4.2
Networks trained from scratch
Composites. A part is a latent z ∈R8 rendered as an 8 × 8 glyph by a ﬁxed random nonlinear generator, and a
composite of two parts is rendered on an 8 × 16 canvas under one of three relations (Figure 4a): BESIDE places the
glyphs in disjoint regions; OVER draws both in the same region, the second mirrored; and OCCLUDE draws the second
glyph at half scale over the brightest quadrant of the ﬁrst, so that where a part goes depends on its host. BESIDE and
OVER are exactly additive in the rendered pixels; OCCLUDE is not (interaction 12–16% of composite variance, mean
14%). BESIDE and OVER are invertible in the latents; under OCCLUDE the latents are plausibly recoverable, but the
occluded pixels of the host are not.
Task and systems. An encoder sees only a composite and writes a bottleneck state h ∈Rm; a decoder receives h
and a ﬂag for a target relation and must render the same parts, in the same roles, under that relation. The state h is
therefore the system’s representation of the composite, and the decoder is the system’s own procedure for reading it.
We compare an MLP, a CNN, and a transformer over pixel columns, each with an MLP decoder, and a role–ﬁller
decoder that splits h into two slots, decodes each with one shared glyph decoder, and renders the relation with a ﬁxed
renderer. A quarter of the vocabulary never occupies role 2 in training (a novel-role split in the spirit of Hadley’s strong
systematicity, 1994, without embedding), and a random ﬁfth of the remaining pairs is withheld as a difﬁculty-matched
baseline. We vary m ∈{8, 16, 32, 64} around kq = 16, the dimension of a pair of latents, and V ∈{8, 16, 32, 64},
with 9 seeds each.
Readouts. Ridge readouts map h to each role’s glyph. A population readout is ﬁtted on 2,000 composites of a
pool of 400 glyphs that the network never saw, which estimates Deﬁnition 1; a vocabulary readout is ﬁtted on the
network’s training composites, as a probing study would; and the population readout is also ﬁtted to the state of the
same network before training. All three are evaluated on composites of a further 256 fresh glyphs. We also compute
the Eckart–Young ceiling of Proposition 4 from the principal components of glyph pixels.
Results. Every architecture reproduces unseen pairs of training glyphs well, with output R2 between 0.85 and 1.00 in
every condition except the transformer trained on eight glyphs (0.53–0.61; Figure 4b, dashed). Behavior on held-out
pairs therefore barely separates the systems. Behavior on fresh glyphs does, and it is governed by the number of
distinct training glyphs: with m = 64, output R2 rises from 0.20–0.34 at V = 8 (below zero for the transformer) to
0.85–0.91 at V = 64 (Figure 4b, solid).
The states tell a different story (Figure 4c). The population readout recovers fresh glyphs from the states of the MLP,
CNN, and role–ﬁller networks with R2 between 0.83 and 0.86 already at V = 8, and 0.89 at V = 64; the same readout
recovers 0.82–0.83 from the untrained networks and 0.91 from the input composite itself. Against the references of
Deﬁnition 2 at the same width, concatenated glyphs projected to m = 64 dimensions (0.98) and summed glyphs (0.48),
the trained states score κ ≈0.82; relative to what the input makes linearly available, they lose almost nothing. By
Deﬁnition 1, then, the states of these networks are recoverable whatever their vocabulary, largely because they inherit
an invertible input. What the small vocabulary breaks is the networks’ use of their own states. A readout ﬁtted on the
network’s own training composites recovers only 0.21–0.24 at V = 8, and it predicts the network’s own fresh-glyph
behavior across runs with r = 0.96 (r = 0.64 within conditions), against r = 0.73 (0.40 within conditions) for the
population readout; the difference between the within-condition correlations has a 95% bootstrap interval of 0.14 to
0.36 (Figure 4d). The vocabulary readout does not predict behavior merely because it shares the network’s vocabulary:
the same readout ﬁtted on the raw input composites of that vocabulary correlates with behavior at only r = 0.10 within
conditions, and adds nothing once the trained-state readout is known. The network’s decoder behaves like a readout
ﬁtted on its vocabulary, consistent with the mechanism of Proposition 5. These networks therefore occupy the upper
right cell of Table 1: the parts are in the state, and the system fails to read them, a failure of performance. The
transformer is the exception: training on few glyphs leaves its state less recoverable (0.59 at V = 8) though more than
its untrained state (0.41).
Two controls show that distinct parts matter beyond combinations. Holding the number of training pairs at 140 while
drawing them from 16, 32, or 64 glyphs raises fresh-glyph behavior of the MLP, CNN, and role–ﬁller networks from
0.47–0.64 to 0.81–0.82 (the transformer’s from 0.32 to 0.45), while behavior on held-out pairs falls by 0.05–0.10
(by 0.32 for the transformer); with all 2,420 pairs of 64 glyphs, fresh-glyph behavior is a further 0.04–0.09 higher
(Figure 4e). Adding an auxiliary loss that reconstructs each glyph from the state raises fresh-glyph behavior at V = 8
by at most 0.14 (Appendix D). Width matters only below kq: at m = 8 the population readout reaches 0.67–0.71
against an Eckart–Young ceiling of 0.79, and from m = 16 on it stays 0.08–0.14 below the ceiling (Figure 4f).

Method. We declare a part code e that does not depend on the system: the top 16 principal components of glyph
pixels, which carry 98% of their variance. On composites of the 400-glyph pool we ﬁt the role-factorized model of
Eq. (2) by ridge regression and a ridge readout ˆei of each role’s code. Each test of Proposition 7 then replaces a
code by the corresponding prediction and is evaluated on fresh glyphs. Reversal predicts h(b, a) from the single state

never occupied role 2, using x’s code read from a composite in which it occupies role 1. The interchange intervention
edits h(a, b) by B1(ˆe1(h(a′, b′)) −ˆe1(h(a, b))), with a donor that shares neither glyph, which by Proposition 7(iv)

untouched half of the decoded BESIDE output divided by the mean change in the edited half, and compare it with the
same intervention on role 2 and with three edits of the same norm: a random direction, a random direction inside the
range of B1, and the natural direction h(a′, b′) −h(a, b), which changes both parts. Each predicted or edited state is
passed to the network’s own decoder. Finally, we ﬁne-tune each network for 200 steps on ﬁve composites of a fresh

role structure: the same ﬁne-tuning on a training glyph, and ﬁne-tuning on the fresh glyph in both roles.

role–ﬁller networks and 0.06–0.13 for the transformer. The predicted reversals match the true reversed states with R2
of 0.96 to 0.99 (0.84–0.92 for the transformer), and the decoder renders them as well as it renders the true reversed
states, 0.86 against 0.86 at V = 64 and 0.48 against 0.49 at V = 16 (Figure 5a). Placing a glyph in a new role
from its code read elsewhere reproduces the network’s own output for that composite, 0.81 against 0.81 at V = 64
(Figure 5b). At V = 64, the interchange intervention on role 1 renders the donor glyph with R2 = 0.78, against
0.81 when the network is given a composite containing the donor itself, and keeps the other glyph at 0.78 against 0.80
without intervention; its spill is 0.16. The intervention on role 2 is equally local (spill 0.19). Edits of the same norm
that do not follow a role’s code are not: the spill is 0.90 for the natural direction, 0.75 for a random direction inside
the range of B1, and 0.96 for a random direction (Figure 5c). Locality is therefore a property of the role components
that the readouts identify, not of any edit in the range of B1. At V = 16 the spill of the role-1 intervention is larger

by the readouts are thus the ones the decoder uses: for these networks, the recovered constituents make the selective

the states are nearly role-factorized, this result is close to what Proposition 7(iv) predicts, and the informative quantity
is the spill. Under OCCLUDE the states are less factorized (δ = 0.11–0.22, up to 0.34 for the transformer), predicted

states nevertheless decode better than the network’s own states (0.76 against 0.68 at V = 64), which suggests that the
network’s encoder, not the readout, is the less systematic of the two.

Recursion
Composites. Balanced binary trees of depth n have leaves drawn from a vocabulary of V Gaussian parts in R8, or
fresh.
Systems and readouts. A recursive auto-associative memory (Pollack, 1990) applies one learned composer Rw ×
Rw →Rw at every node and one learned decomposer recursively, trained end to end on trees of depth 1 and 2.
We compare HRR of the same width with one pair of unitary role vectors per level of the tree, which is needed
because circular convolution commutes and a single pair would let roles cancel across levels; its leaves are decoded
by recursive unbinding and identiﬁed by clean-up. Each system thus has its own decoding procedure. For both, we
also ﬁt a population readout of Deﬁnition 1: a ridge map from the depth-n state to all 2n leaves, ﬁtted on 6,000 trees
of fresh leaves and evaluated on 2,000 others.
Results. We ran 5 seeds for each width w ∈{16, . . . , 256} and V ∈{16, 64} (Figure 6a,b). Within the trained
depths the learned composer’s own decomposer recovers fresh leaves almost perfectly whenever w ≥32 (R2 ≥0.95;
at w = 16, depth 1 reaches only 0.56–0.76). At depth 3 it fails even with ample width: at w = 256, where the
ceiling of Proposition 9 is still 1, fresh-leaf R2 is 0.26 and familiar leaves are identiﬁed only 49% of the time with
V = 16. The population readout shows that the failure lies in the decomposer, not the state: it recovers fresh leaves
from the composer’s depth-3 state with R2 = 0.99 at w = 256, and at every width and depth it stays within 0.13
of the ceiling min(1, w/(2nd)), including depths 4 and 5, at which the composer was never trained. The composer’s
relation remains invertible on its own outputs; its decomposer, learned at depths 1 and 2, does not extend one level
deeper. HRR is the mirror case. A population readout recovers its leaves exactly (R2 ≥0.999) whenever w ≥2nd, as
Proposition 3 requires for a linear relation of full rank, while recursive unbinding by correlation, which treats crosstalk
between superposed bindings as noise, loses about (2n −1)d/w of the variance: at w = 256 its R2 falls from 0.96 at
depth 1 to 0.75 at depth 3 and −0.13 at depth 5, against 0.97, 0.78, and 0.03 predicted by that formula, and clean-up
identiﬁes familiar leaves with accuracy 1.00, 0.97, and 0.66. Under Deﬁnition 2 both relations are productive up to
the width ceiling; the procedures the two systems use to read them are not, and the depth curve shows how each falls
short.
4.5
Pretrained text encoders
Composites. Sentences place two nouns in the roles of ﬁve asymmetric relations, such as “a x on a y” and “a x to the
left of a y”, and of the control “a x and a y”. We use 305 concrete nouns, split at random into 165 for ﬁtting readouts,
40 for validation, and 100 as fresh parts. No noun is fresh to the models; the fresh nouns are fresh to the readout, the
second sense of Section 3.
Systems and readouts. We examine the text encoders of three CLIP models (Radford et al., 2021), including the
one that conditions Stable Diffusion 1.5 (Rombach et al., 2022); T5-small, -base, and -large (Raffel et al., 2020);
BERT-base and -large (Devlin et al., 2019); and GPT-2 small, medium, and large (Radford et al., 2019). We consider
three declared part codes, the state of “a x”, the state of “there is a x here”, and the input embedding, each reduced
to 16 principal components, and states mean-pooled at four depths or taken from the pooled output. The part-code
components are ﬁtted on the ﬁtting nouns only. Ridge readouts map the composite state to each role’s part code; the
ridge penalty, the layer, and the part code are chosen on the validation nouns, so that no choice uses a composite of a
reported noun and penalties are not tuned to composites that share nouns. To calibrate, we apply the identical pipeline
to two reference operators built from each model’s own part codes: concatenation, which is role–ﬁller, and the sum,
a symmetric blend, and we report the normalized score κ of Deﬁnition 2. As a selectivity control (Hewitt and Liang,
2019), we run the same pipeline on each architecture with random weights and the same tokenizer.
Results. We ran 3 seeds per model, each a random split of the nouns, and report means over the asymmetric relations.

with 0.46 to 0.50, so the method separates the two modes with these part codes. Every model recovers nouns from
new pairs of ﬁtting nouns with R2 between 0.58 and 0.98 (Figure 6c). On fresh nouns the models spread out. The
three GPT-2 models reach 0.90–0.92 (κ = 0.81–0.83) and T5-base 0.82 (κ = 0.66), far above the same architectures

5
Discussion
We proposed that a representation is compositional when it combines parts through a relation from which every part,
including a part never seen before, can be read back in its role by a ﬁxed procedure of a declared kind, and when
each part enters every role through one shared code. Formally, the relation is recoverable and role-factorized over the
distribution of parts, relative to declared readouts and declared methods for ﬁnding parts, and it is estimated on parts
that played no role in ﬁtting the readouts. Measuring recovery over the part distribution, on parts not used for ﬁtting,
is what gives the deﬁnition content: on a ﬁnite vocabulary nearly every mode of combination can be decoded, and
the evidence usually offered for compositional representations, recovery of familiar parts and generalization to unseen
pairs of them, is satisﬁed by systems whose states lose their parts.
Our experiments also show what the deﬁnition does not settle by itself. The states of small networks trained on glyph
composites were recoverable, and nearly role-factorized, whatever their training, largely because an invertible input
survives a random encoder; what training on few glyphs failed to produce was a decoder that could use the state for
new glyphs. The learned recursive composer showed the same split across depth: its state kept every leaf one and two
levels beyond its training, while its decomposer did not. In the pretrained encoders, by contrast, training itself made
the difference for GPT-2 and T5 and none for CLIP. A representational criterion and a criterion on the system’s own
procedures are therefore both needed, and they can come apart in either direction (Table 1).
What a network must do. Our results suggest ﬁve requirements, each tied to a proposition and an experiment. First,
the composite state must have room for every part: a state narrower than the parts it combines loses them, and an
afﬁne readout can recover at most the share of their variance that its width allows (Propositions 3 and 4; Figures 3b
and 4f). Second, the system’s own procedure for reading parts must be learned from many distinct parts, not many
combinations: a network trained on eight glyphs solves unseen pairs and fails on new glyphs although its state contains
them, because its decoder, like any procedure ﬁtted to composites, is determined only where its training composites
constrain it (Proposition 5; Figure 4b–e). Coverage of combinations, which benchmarks reward, matters less than the
number of distinct parts. Third, parts should enter every role through one code: when they do, reversals and new role
assignments are ﬁxed by single composites, and the networks’ own decoders render the predicted states as well as their
true ones (Proposition 7; Figure 5a,b). Fourth, the role components must be the ones the system uses: replacing one
12

role’s component with that of another composite changes the output for that role and leaves the other nearly untouched
(spill 0.16 against 0.90 for a natural change of the same size; Figure 5c), although this holds for in-distribution changes
only. Fifth, the relation must stay invertible on its own outputs, and the system’s procedure must follow it: the learned
composer’s relation stayed invertible beyond its training depths, but its decomposer, learned at depths 1 and 2, failed
one level deeper (Figure 6a). A network that meets all ﬁve, veriﬁed on fresh parts against references built from its
own part codes, has earned the term in our sense; one that meets only the behavioral tests should be described as
generalizing compositionally.
Terminology. Behavioral evidence should be reported as compositional generalization, with the split it was measured
on; the bare term should be reserved for systems whose parts are recoverable from composites of fresh parts and
whose own procedures use them. Reports of recovered parts should state the readout class and whether the parts
were among those used to ﬁt the readout; a readout ﬁtted on a network’s training vocabulary measures the reach of a
vocabulary-trained procedure, not what the state contains.
Architecture and learning. Fodor and McLaughlin (1990) asked that systematicity follow from the architecture. Our
criterion is a property of states and procedures, and a trained network can come to satisfy it; within such a network,
the states ﬁx what every arrangement of known parts should look like, but whether the network learns such states and
a decoder that uses them remains contingent on training, as the seed spread at small vocabularies shows. Learning is
contingent in a second way: ﬁne-tuning on a new part in one role damaged that part in the other role and damaged old
pairs, although a role-factorized state allows a part to be added by adding its code alone (Figure 5d). Architectures
that build in role-factorized slots move part of the guarantee into the architecture, but our role–ﬁller decoder shows
that the encoder must still learn a recoverable relation and the decoder must still see enough distinct parts. Whether
any training regime reliably leads a network without built-in roles to a role-factorized relation under overlapping roles,
and to learning that respects it, is open.
People. Human evidence shows role-separated codes and recombination of known parts, but almost none of it was
gathered with parts that are fresh in our sense. Agent and patient are decoded from separate cortical codes that gen-
eralize to new sentences, but over a closed set of nouns and verbs (Frankland and Greene, 2015); people represent
novel objects as compositions of known elements (Schwartenbeck et al., 2023), use a newly learned word in combi-
nations they have never seen (Lake et al., 2019), and endorse inferences across arbitrary contents (Rips, 1994). Novel
noun–noun compounds are often interpreted by linking the two concepts through a thematic relation, a role–ﬁller
mode; often by mapping one property of the modiﬁer onto the head, which keeps the head and only a fragment of the
modiﬁer; and less often by hybridizing them, a blend (Wisniewski, 1997); and belief overrides validity (Evans et al.,
1983). A human analogue of our test would teach a new word, object, or face and ask whether it can be read back from
a scene or sentence in a role it was never shown in. The paradigms that come closest are the wug test (Berko, 1958),
rule learning over novel syllables (Marcus et al., 1999), and syntactic bootstrapping of novel verbs (Gleitman, 1990;
Naigles, 1990); illusory conjunctions (Treisman and Schmidt, 1982) and binding limits in working memory (Wheeler
and Treisman, 2002) suggest that human recovery of fresh conjunctions is capacity-limited and graded. Appendix C
collects this evidence into a reference proﬁle against which systems can be compared.
Future work and limitations. Our trained networks are small and our domains synthetic, chosen so that every state
could be examined and fresh parts could be drawn; the pretrained encoders were probed with three declared part
codes and a linear readout, on nouns that were fresh only to the readout, and a richer class M, including discovered
tensor-product decompositions (Soulos et al., 2020) or contextual token states, might ﬁnd a decomposition we did
not. Can unsupervised methods reliably discover the part codes of a large network, so that a negative result reﬂects
the network and not the method? Does scale, which brings decodability of constituents (Redhardt et al., 2025), also
bring recovery of fresh parts? And can a system keep its parts recoverable while matching networks in perception
and graded similarity? Structured systems that build in parts of the criterion, such as DORA (Doumas et al., 2022),
CHREST (Gobet and Simon, 2000), and TRELLIS (Singaravadivelan and Langley, 2026), are natural next candidates
for the same tests.

Appendix B
Modes of combination
Table 2 classiﬁes widely discussed modes of combination by the properties of Deﬁnition 1. In the selective modiﬁ-
cation model, the modiﬁer changes the values and weights of those attributes of the head to which it applies (Smith
et al., 1988); in composite prototypes, attribute conﬂicts are resolved and emergent attributes appear according to the

and Simon, 1984), whereas CHREST adds templates with slots for variable ﬁllers (Gobet and Simon, 1996b, 2000);

The generator maps z ∈R8 through a random tanh layer of 48 units and a spatially smoothed linear map to 8 × 8

D.3 Recursion
Leaves are N(0, I8). The composer and decomposer are MLPs with one hidden layer of 256 tanh units, with learned
linear maps between R8 and Rw; training uses 6,000 steps of Adam on batches of 128 trees, alternating depths 1 and
2. HRR lifts leaves to Rw by a random linear map, binds with one pair of unitary role vectors per level of the tree, and
reads leaves by recursive circular correlation and the pseudo-inverse of the lift; identiﬁcation is by nearest neighbor
among V candidates.
D.4 Pretrained encoders
Sentence states are mean-pooled over tokens at layers L/4, L/2, 3L/4, and L, together with the pooled output (the
end-of-text state for CLIP and the last token otherwise). For each relation and seed, the 305 nouns are split at random
into 165 ﬁtting, 40 validation, and 100 fresh nouns; 3,000 sentences are sampled from pairs of ﬁtting nouns for ﬁtting,
800 further pairs of ﬁtting nouns for the new-pairs test, 600 pairs of validation nouns, and 1,000 pairs of fresh nouns.
Each part code is reduced to 16 principal components ﬁtted on the 165 ﬁtting nouns. Ridge readouts take the penalty
from 17 values between 10−3 and 105 that maximizes R2 on the validation nouns, so the penalty, like the layer
and code, is chosen on nouns the readout was not ﬁtted on. The random-weight control loads each architecture and
tokenizer with freshly initialized weights (seed 0) and runs the same protocol. The pool-size curve ﬁts the readouts on
the ﬁrst 40, 80, 120, or 165 ﬁtting nouns, with validation and fresh nouns unchanged. Layer and part code are chosen
per model to maximize mean R2 on validation nouns over the asymmetric relations. Identiﬁcation is by cosine nearest
neighbor among the 100 fresh nouns. The reversal similarity is the cosine between the states of a sentence and its role
reversal, normalized so that 0 is the similarity of unrelated sentences and 1 is identity.
