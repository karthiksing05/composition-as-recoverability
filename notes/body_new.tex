\begin{abstract}
Anyone who knows the words \emph{cake} and \emph{plate} understands ``a plate under a cake'' on first hearing and can say which object is on top. Compositionality names this ability, and it comes in degrees. We describe compositionality as a spectrum of capabilities and divide the spectrum into two forms, one containing the other. Weak compositionality combines known parts into new wholes and acts correctly on them. Strong compositionality keeps every weak capability and can also take each whole apart into its parts in their roles, so a strongly compositional system handles parts it never combined, places a new part in every role, adds parts without forgetting and nests wholes. We call this second ability decomposition and measure it by recoverability, a calibrated score of how well a simple readout declared in advance returns parts held out from its fit. These terms give a shared language that places classical, connectionist, vector-symbolic, disentanglement and benchmark accounts of compositionality on one spectrum. We hypothesize that behavioral compositionality is a subset of representational compositionality, able to do some, and not all, of the things representational compositionality can do. We prove that a system's state bounds the behavior that reads it, and that tests on training parts cannot detect decomposition. Across trained networks and pretrained language, text and image models, behavior never exceeds the decomposition its states support outside a limit of our readouts, it often lags that decomposition, and procedures that read recoverable states lift behavior from the weak form to the strong one.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Composition is the study of parts and their combination through some operation. A compositional system builds wholes from reusable parts, so the meaning of a sentence follows from the meanings of its words and the way they combine \citep{frege1923,janssen1997}, and a scene follows from its objects and their places.

Composition matters because a finite experience must support an unbounded number of new situations. People understand sentences they have never heard \citep{chomsky1957}, and a person who learns the verb \emph{dax} immediately understands \emph{dax twice} \citep{lake2018}. Composition earns its value through the abilities that follow from reuse \citep{lake2017}. A compositional system handles new combinations of known parts, takes in a new part and uses it in every role, adds knowledge without damaging old knowledge, nests wholes inside wholes, and keeps each part in its role.

Wholes differ in how much of their parts they keep (Figure~\ref{fig:intro}). An avocado placed on an armchair can be lifted off again, while an armchair shaped like an avocado keeps neither object whole. Two photographs side by side keep their places, and their pixel average mixes them. A sentence keeps each noun in its role in either word order. Building a whole is composition, and taking the whole back apart into the same parts in the same roles is decomposition.

An avocado-shaped armchair and the pixel average of two photographs merge the parts, and many pairs of parts give the same whole (red).   \label{fig:intro}

Today's tests score composition through behavior. Benchmarks ask a model to act on new combinations of training parts \citep{lake2018,keysers2020,hupkes2020}, and text-to-image models compose concepts into new scenes \citep{ramesh2021}. These tests reward recombination and miss failures elsewhere. A lookup over a finite vocabulary passes them, success can come from fragments seen in training \citep{dziri2023}, and text-to-image models misplace objects \citep{conwell2022} and attach an attribute to the wrong object \citep{rassin2022}.

We propose that compositionality is a spectrum of capabilities, and that the spectrum divides into two forms, one containing the other. \emph{Weak compositionality} combines known parts into new wholes and acts correctly on them. \emph{Strong compositionality} keeps every weak capability and adds the capabilities that need the wholes to come apart. A strongly compositional system handles parts it never combined, places a new part in every role, adds parts without damaging old ones and nests wholes inside wholes. Every strongly compositional system is therefore weakly compositional, and a weakly compositional system can lack decomposition entirely.

The two notions form a weak and a strong version of composition. Behavioral compositionality is the weak version, in which a system composes parts, and any decomposition it performs comes from a procedure trained for that purpose. Representational compositionality is the strong version, in which a system composes parts and its composites also decompose. Recoverability is our heuristic for the strong version, and it asks whether parts read out easily from the internal states of a composite. Decomposition matters because it supports the efficient use of parts and reveals what each part donates to the whole.

Decomposition needs a careful definition, because a loose one holds for every system. A lookup table over the parts seen in training inverts any whole that is injective on them, and an unrestricted probe decodes almost anything \citep{hewitt2019}. Two conditions give decomposition content. The procedure that takes a whole apart must come from a simple class declared in advance, and it must succeed on parts held out from its fit. Recoverability scores decomposition under these two conditions.

We hypothesize that behavioral compositionality is a subset of representational compositionality, able to do some, and not all, of the things representational compositionality can do. A system whose own procedures handle held-out parts has a state that holds them, while a state can hold parts that its system's procedures miss. A recoverable state therefore marks capabilities that a procedure reading it can reach, before anyone builds that procedure.

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A spectrum of compositional capabilities.} We define weak and strong compositionality by the capabilities each supports, with strong compositionality containing weak compositionality and adding decomposition.
  \item \textbf{Decomposition and its measure.} We define decomposition under a declared readout class on held-out parts and measure it by recoverability, with a score $\kappa$ that runs from the sum of the part codes (0) to their concatenation (1).
  \item \textbf{Limits in theory.} We prove that tests on training parts cannot detect decomposition, that a system's state bounds the behavior that reads it, and that the rank of a relation and the width and depth of a state cap recoverability.
  \item \textbf{A shared language.} We place fifteen accounts of compositionality, from classical constituents to disentanglement and benchmarks, on one spectrum of demands on decomposition.
  \item \textbf{Evidence across systems.} In networks trained from scratch and pretrained models with up to 15 billion parameters, behavior lags the decomposition the states support, procedures that read recoverable states repair each failure of behavior, and pretrained models build recoverability beyond their inputs.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Compositionality of meaning.} Frege's principle holds that the meaning of a complex follows from the meanings of its parts and the way they combine \citep{frege1923}, and \citet{montague1970} made it a homomorphism from syntax to semantics \citep[see][]{janssen1997}. As a constraint on functions alone the principle is vacuous, because a recoding of the parts makes any assignment of meanings compositional \citep{zadrozny1994}. Conceptual combination strains it from the other side. A guppy is a typical pet fish and neither a typical pet nor a typical fish \citep{osherson1981}, and conceptual blending builds wholes with structure of their own \citep{fauconnier2002}.

\textbf{Behavioral tests of composition.} The debate over composition in networks began with connectionist models of language \citep{rumelhart1986,pinker1988,elman1990}, and \citet{marcus1998} argued that networks fail to extend a relation outside their training space. Benchmarks score a model's outputs on new combinations of training parts \citep{lake2018,keysers2020}, \citet{hupkes2020} divide the property into five tests, and meta-learning brings networks to the human level \citep{lake2023}. Diffusion models trained on a random hierarchy learn its rules level by level \citep{favero2025diffusion}. These tests score outputs and leave the states unscored.

\textbf{Constituent structure.} \citet{fodor1988} argued that the systematicity and productivity of thought require representations whose constituents are tokened in every whole that contains them. \citet{smolensky1990} answered with tensor-product representations, which bind fillers to roles. Linearly independent roles let a linear operation return every filler exactly, although the state holds no explicit copy of it. \citet{fodor1990} replied that constituents that are not tokened cannot act in processing, and the dispute continues \citep{goodale2026}.

\textbf{Context-dependent constituents.} \citet{smolensky1988} also described a looser kind of constituent. Subtracting \emph{cup without coffee} from \emph{cup with coffee} leaves coffee in the context of cup, a constituent that differs in the context of can or tree and comes apart only approximately. He called the two kinds strong and weak compositional structure \citep{smolensky1987}, a distinction among representations that differs from our distinction among capabilities, and \emph{red} likewise contributes differently to \emph{red hair} and \emph{red apple} \citep{kamp1995}.

\textbf{Procedures that build and decompose.} \citet{vangelder1990} called representations functionally compositional when general procedures build and decompose them. Reduced descriptions compressed part--whole hierarchies into single patterns \citep{hinton1990}, recursive auto-associative memories learned the procedures \citep{pollack1990}, and vector-symbolic architectures fixed them \citep{plate1995,gayler2006,kanerva2009}. Models of analogy bind fillers to roles explicitly \citep{hummel1997}, and binding remains a problem for perception and for networks \citep{treisman1980,greff2020}.

\textbf{Reading parts from representations.} Probes decode constituents \citep{belinkov2022}, tensor-product decompositions \citep{mccoy2019} and interchange interventions \citep{geiger2021} supply part codes, and superposition lets a state carry more features than dimensions \citep{elhage2022}. Disentanglement seeks codes that separate the factors of variation \citep{bengio2013,locatello2019}, and identifiability results guarantee generalization for additive decoders \citep{lachapelle2023,wiedemer2024}. Decodable factors need not yield a decoder that generalizes \citep{montero2021}. We add parts held out from the readout and compare the readout with the system's own procedures.

\textbf{Measures of compositional structure.} Compression measures ask whether a composition of part codes reproduces a representation \citep{andreas2019}. \citet{elmoznino2024} define representational compositionality as the compressibility of a representation into a short semantics over discrete sentences. \citet{elmoznino2026} score the structure a whole reveals beyond its parts, $K(m_x \mid m_{x_a}, m_{x_b})$, which is near zero when the structure of the whole is recoverable from the structures of its parts. Both score the direction from parts to whole on the data themselves. We score the direction from whole to parts, through a declared readout of a system's state on held-out parts.

\section{Weak and strong compositionality}
\label{sec:definition}

In this section, we define the two forms of compositionality by their capabilities. Weak compositionality recombines known parts. Decomposition takes wholes apart under a simple, declared class of procedures, and strong compositionality joins it to the weak form. We then relate the two forms to behavior and to representation. Appendix~\ref{app:proofs} gives the proofs.

\subsection{Parts, composites and roles}
\label{sec:intuition}

A part is a draw from a distribution $\D$ over possible parts, such as glyphs, nouns or photographs. A system assigns each part $x$ a \emph{part code} $e(x) \in \R^d$, and it assigns a composite $r(x_1, \ldots, x_k)$ of $k$ parts in the roles of a relation $r$ a state $h = g_r(x_1, \ldots, x_k) \in \R^m$. Training exposes a system to a finite \emph{vocabulary} $\Voc$ of \emph{training} parts. A part is \emph{held out} from a procedure when no composite containing it helped fit the procedure. A glyph can be held out both from a readout and from the network that encodes it, while a noun in a pretrained model can be held out only from the readout.

Suppose a system adds two part vectors $x$ and $y$. Every pair on the line $x + y = z$ yields the same composite, so no procedure can return the parts. A tensor-product binding $x \otimes \rho_1 + y \otimes \rho_2$ with independent role vectors \citep{smolensky1990} keeps one decomposition for each composite. A small vocabulary hides the difference, because few vocabulary pairs share a line and a lookup finds the summed parts. A held-out pair exposes it, because its line holds infinitely many other decompositions.

\subsection{Weak compositionality recombines known parts}
\label{sec:notions}

Weak compositionality asks a system to act correctly on new combinations of parts it has seen. A decoder renders a new pair of training glyphs, a language model answers a question about a new sentence of familiar nouns, and a benchmark scores a new command built from training words.

\begin{defbox}{Weak compositionality}{behcomp}
A system is \emph{weakly compositional} with respect to a relation $r$ to the degree that its procedures, such as a decoder, a decomposer or its answers to questions about the roles, give the correct output for new composites of training parts.
\end{defbox}

Weak compositionality asks nothing of the way back from whole to parts. A non-invertible relation can be injective on a finite vocabulary, and a lookup table then inverts it (Lemma~\ref{lem:lookup}), as clean-up does for vector-symbolic codes \citep{plate1995}. Any procedure learned from a vocabulary shares this limit. Composites fix an affine procedure only on the codes they contain, so the procedure covers held-out parts only when the codes of the training parts in each role have full rank $d$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:span}). This result is a procedure-side form of the argument of \citet{marcus1998}.

\subsection{Decomposition makes parts reusable}
\label{sec:decomposition}

A system composes when it builds wholes from parts, and its composites decompose when a procedure can take each whole apart into the same parts in the same roles. Decomposition matters because every later use of a part passes through it (Table~\ref{tab:capabilities}).
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Held-out parts.} A whole that comes apart exposes each part, including parts its system never combined.
  \item \textbf{New parts in every role.} A part learned in one role enters another, because the part and its role come apart.
  \item \textbf{Additions without forgetting.} A new part enters through its own code, so old wholes stay intact.
  \item \textbf{Nesting.} A composite that decomposes can serve as a part, so wholes nest inside wholes.
  \item \textbf{Inspection.} A decomposition shows the share each part donates to the whole, and an edit of that share changes one part alone.
\end{itemize}

Decomposition alone comes cheap. A lookup table decomposes any composite that is injective on a finite vocabulary, an unrestricted probe decodes almost anything \citep{hewitt2019}, and a recoding of the parts makes any assignment of meanings compositional \citep{zadrozny1994}. Two conditions give decomposition content.

\begin{defbox}{Decomposition and strong compositionality}{decomp}
The composites of a relation $r$ \emph{decompose} under a declared class $\U$ of procedures if, for every role $i$, one procedure $u_i \in \U$, fitted on composites of some parts, returns the part in role $i$ from composites of parts held out from its fit. A system is \emph{strongly compositional} with respect to $r$ when it is weakly compositional and its composites decompose.
\end{defbox}

The class makes decomposition simple, and the held-out parts make it general. A procedure from a small class cannot memorize a vocabulary, so its success on held-out parts shows that the parts survive in the state. Every strongly compositional system is weakly compositional by definition, and the capabilities of Table~\ref{tab:capabilities} separate the two forms.

\subsection{Behavior and representation}
\label{sec:subset}

A system can show either form in two places. Its own procedures show it in behavior, and its states show it in representation. A behavioral test scores the system's outputs. A representational test scores a state through a readout that the experimenter declares, so it scores decomposition directly.

Behavioral compositionality is a subset of representational compositionality. Whenever a system's own procedure returns the parts of held-out composites, the state it reads holds those parts, and that procedure witnesses decomposition under any readout class that contains it (Proposition~\ref{prop:subset}). The inclusion is strict, because a state can hold parts that the system's own procedures miss. A system can therefore act with the weak form while its states support the strong form, and a new procedure that reads those states can reach the strong capabilities. For a fixed readout class, behavior can still outrun the declared readouts, which marks a limit of the class.

This relation restates an old dispute. \citet{smolensky1990} built states that return their constituents without containing them explicitly, and decomposition adopts this notion, because it asks only that a declared procedure return each part \citep[cf.][]{vangelder1990}. \citet{fodor1990} objected that constituents that are not tokened cannot act in processing. A decomposable state alone leaves this objection open, and interchange edits test it directly (Section~\ref{sec:success}).

\section{Recoverability measures decomposition}
\label{sec:repcomp}

In this section, we turn decomposition into a number, describe the structure that makes decomposition simple, give the results that limit it, and show on analytic operators that held-out parts separate decomposition from blends.

\subsection{Recoverability}

A \emph{readout class} $\U$, such as affine maps, contains the procedures allowed to recover a part's code from a state. We fix the class in advance, because when the coordinates of an additive decomposition are unrestricted, every invertible relation is additive (Lemma~\ref{lem:psi}).

\begin{defbox}{Recoverable relations}{recoverable}
Fix a part distribution $\D$, a part code $e$, and a readout class $\U$. A relation $r$, which a system realizes by $g_r$, is \emph{$\varepsilon$-recoverable} if for every role $i$ some $u_i \in \U$ satisfies, for parts $x_1, \ldots, x_k$ drawn independently from $\D$,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\mathbb{E}\,\lVert u_i(g_r(x_1,\ldots,x_k)) - e(x_i) \rVert^2}{\mathbb{E}\,\lVert e(x) - \mathbb{E}\,e(x) \rVert^2} \;\le\; \varepsilon .
  \label{eq:recover}
\end{equation}
We estimate this quantity by fitting $u_i$ on composites of a \emph{fitting pool} of parts and evaluating it on composites of other parts, with errors in a declared inner product on part codes.
\end{defbox}

\begin{defbox}{Representational compositionality}{repcomp}
A system is \emph{representationally compositional} with respect to a relation $r$, relative to a declared readout class $\U$ and a declared class $\M$ of methods for finding part codes, to the degree that its realization of $r$ is $\varepsilon$-recoverable for some part code $e$ found under $\M$ that is informative about the parts (Definition~\ref{def:informative}). We report the degree by
\begin{equation}
  \kappa = \frac{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{blend}} - \varepsilon}{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{blend}} - \varepsilon_{\mathrm{ref}}},
  \label{eq:kappa}
\end{equation}
where the same procedure gives $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ref}}$ for the concatenation and $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{blend}}$ for the sum of the same part codes. The system is representationally compositional at tolerance $\tau$ if $\kappa \ge 1 - \tau$ for some such $e$ informative at $\tau$.
\end{defbox}

\begin{defbox}{Informative part codes and productivity}{informative}
A part code $e$ is \emph{informative} at tolerance $\tau$ if an affine map of $e$ recovers a declared description $z(x)$ of the parts that does not depend on the system, with $R^2 \ge 1 - \tau$ on held-out parts. A relation is \emph{productive to depth $n$} if Definition~\ref{def:repcomp} holds when the parts are themselves composites of depth up to $n - 1$, with the state of each composite as its part code.
\end{defbox}

Recoverability asks a fixed procedure of a declared kind to return each part in its role across the whole part distribution, and $1 - \varepsilon$ is the population $R^2$ of the best readout in $\U$. A relation fails recoverability when it discards information about its parts. For a symmetric relation of identically distributed parts, no readout can tell the roles apart, so in population $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{blend}} = 1/2$, $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{ref}} = 0$ and $\kappa = 1 - 2\varepsilon$. The references correct the estimates for finite samples and readout fitting.

The declarations close two loopholes. Unrestricted readouts let any injective system pass, as unrestricted probes do \citep{hewitt2019}, so we fix the class. The blend $(Px, Py)$, with $P$ a projection of rank $r < d$, discards $d - r$ dimensions of each part and is exactly recoverable with the code $e(x) = Px$, so we require an informative code. We report informativeness wherever a description of the parts exists apart from the system, which holds for glyph latents and fails for nouns and photographs.

Recoverability differs from decomposition by a system as an ability differs from an act. A system decomposes when one of its own procedures returns the parts. Its states are recoverable when a fixed, simple class of procedures would return them. The score $\kappa$ places a state between a blend and a role--filler reference, so a designer can compare architectures, pooling rules and checkpoints before any procedure reads the state.

\subsection{Shared role codes make decomposition simple}
\label{sec:mechanics}

Recoverability describes an end effect, and a state can produce it in several ways. The simplest gives each part one code that enters every whole, changed only by its role.

\begin{defbox}{Role-factorized relations}{factor}
A relation $r$, which a system realizes by $g_r$, is \emph{$\delta$-role-factorized} if, for some code $e'$ of dimension $d'$ of which the part code $e$ is an affine function, some $c \in \R^m$ and injective matrices $B_1, \ldots, B_k \in \R^{m \times d'}$, one per role and shared by all parts, satisfy
\begin{equation}
  g_r(x_1, \ldots, x_k) = c + \textstyle\sum_{i} B_i\, e'(x_i) + \eta(x_1, \ldots, x_k), \qquad \mathbb{E}\,\lVert \eta \rVert^2 \le \delta\, \mathbb{E}\,\lVert g_r - \mathbb{E}\,g_r \rVert^2 .
  \label{eq:factor}
\end{equation}
We estimate $\delta$ by fitting $c$ and $B_i$ on composites of the fitting pool and evaluating them on composites of other parts.
\end{defbox}

A role-factorized state with full-rank role maps returns every part's code by an affine map, and one composite then fixes every role reversal and every assignment of a known part to a new role (Propositions~\ref{prop:systematic} and~\ref{prop:approx}). Role-factorization is the classical demand that a constituent contribute the same thing to every complex containing it \citep{fodor1988}, and a tensor product with independent role vectors meets it with $\delta = 0$ \citep{smolensky1990}. A relation fails role-factorization when a part's contribution depends on its co-parts, as \emph{coffee} does across \emph{cup with coffee} and \emph{tree with coffee} \citep{smolensky1988}. A state that gives each role its own code can be recoverable with a large $\delta$, so $\delta$ diagnoses the mechanism behind recoverability and adds no condition to Definition~\ref{def:repcomp}.

\subsection{Three limits of decomposition}
\label{sec:consequences}

\textbf{Blends lose a computable share of their parts.} A linear relation $g(x_1, \ldots, x_k) = A x$ with $A = [A_1 \cdots A_k]$ gives each composite an affine set of decompositions of dimension $kd - \mathrm{rank}\,A$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:fiber}). For Gaussian parts, no readout of any kind recovers role $i$ with $R^2$ above $\mathrm{tr}(A_i^{\top}(AA^{\top})^{+}A_i)/d$, which is 0.8 for role~1 of $x + 0.5y$ and 0.5 for $x + y$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:ceiling}).

\textbf{Width caps any affine readout.} For any relation and part distribution, an affine readout of an $m$-dimensional state explains at most the share of the parts' variance in the top $m$ principal components of the stacked part codes (Proposition~\ref{prop:ey}).

\textbf{Depth needs invertibility on the relation's own outputs.} A nested linear blend of depth $n$ into a state of width $d$ loses at least $(2^n - 1)d$ dimensions of its leaves (Proposition~\ref{prop:depth}). Recursive tensor products avoid this loss by raising the tensor rank, so their states grow with depth \citep{smolensky1990}.

\subsection{Held-out parts separate decomposition from blends}
\label{sec:calibration}

We first validate the measure on fourteen analytic operators whose decomposability we know in advance. Parts are Gaussian vectors in $\R^{16}$, and the operators combine them in pairs. They include role--filler binding (concatenation and a tensor product), fixed-width superposition (random projections into $0.5$ to $1.5$ times $kd = 32$ dimensions, HRR and MAP binding \citep{plate1995,gayler2006,kanerva2009}, and $x + 0.5y$), symmetric blends ($x + y$ and $x \odot y$), and a random $\tanh$ layer. We fit ridge, least-squares and MLP readouts of the role-1 part on composites of a vocabulary of $V$ parts, with 5 seeds for each $V$.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_measure.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Training parts hide blends, and held-out parts expose them.} \textbf{a.} Given the candidates, a lookup over composites of 32 training parts (open orange) identifies the role-1 part of every operator except the symmetric blend, while an affine readout separates the operators on held-out parts (filled; red marks the blend $x + y$). \textbf{b.} Affine recovery of held-out parts meets the ceiling of Proposition~\ref{prop:ceiling} across analytic operators. Means over 5 seeds.}
  \label{fig:measure}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Tests on training parts cannot see decomposition.} Given the candidates, a lookup identifies the role-1 part of every operator that is injective on the vocabulary with accuracy 1.00, including half-rank projections and HRR, and it fails only for the symmetric blends, 0.52 (Figure~\ref{fig:measure}a). A test on training parts therefore cannot tell role--filler composition from vector-symbolic superposition.

\textbf{Held-out parts recover the known ceilings.} At $V = 128$, linear readouts recover the role--filler operators and full-rank projections with $R^2 = 1.00$, half-rank projections with 0.47, $x + y$ with 0.50, and $x + 0.5y$ with 0.80, equal to its ceiling (Figure~\ref{fig:measure}b). HRR and MAP reach 0.44 and 0.47, close to the same ceiling of $1/2$. Flexible procedures fall further, because they memorize the vocabulary, and an MLP readout recovers 0.11 of the half-rank projection on held-out parts. Every test that follows therefore scores held-out parts under a readout class fixed in advance.

\section{A shared language for compositionality}
\label{sec:spectrum}

In this section, we place existing accounts of compositionality on one spectrum, ordered by the demands each places on decomposition (Figure~\ref{fig:spectrum}). Each account fixes some aspects of a decomposition and leaves others open, and its place follows from the aspects it fixes (Table~\ref{tab:spectrum}).

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_spectrum.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Accounts of compositionality differ in the demands they place on decomposition.} Each card names a rung, its demand, the accounts that make that demand and the systems of this paper that meet it. A blend loses its parts, and weak compositionality leaves their return to the system's own procedure. Strong compositionality adds decomposition by a simple readout declared in advance, and two stricter rungs also fix the form of that decomposition. Colors follow Figure~\ref{fig:protocol}.}
  \label{fig:spectrum}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Five rungs.} A \emph{blend} composes parts and keeps no decomposition, so no procedure returns them. \emph{Weak compositionality} asks the system's own procedure to act on new combinations of training parts. \emph{Strong compositionality} also asks a simple procedure declared in advance to return every part in every role, for parts held out from its fit (Definition~\ref{def:decomp}). \emph{Shared role codes} also require each part to enter every whole through one code that changes only with its role (Definition~\ref{def:factor}). \emph{Tokened constituents} also require that code to be the part itself, copied into the whole \citep{fodor1988}. Each rung keeps the demands of the rungs below it. Strong compositionality fixes the class of the decomposing procedure, shared role codes fix its form, and tokened constituents fix the code.

\paragraph{Placing accounts.} Frege's principle and Montague's homomorphism fix how the meaning of a whole is built and ask nothing of the way back, so they define composition alone. Reverse compositionality adds the way back \citep{fodor2002}, and functional compositionality asks for general procedures that build and decompose every whole \citep{vangelder1990}. Both reach strong compositionality, and our declared class supplies the generality they leave open. Benchmarks, text-to-image models and diffusion models on the random hierarchy score the outputs of the system's own procedure, so they test weak compositionality and cannot tell a lost part from an unread one. Vector-symbolic architectures sit on two rungs. Clean-up against a stored vocabulary decomposes as weak compositionality does, and a readout recovers held-out parts only while the width holds every part. Tensor products, disentangled codes and additive decoders fix one code per role. Compression measures and Kolmogorov scores of compositional structure test the direction from parts to whole on training data \citep{andreas2019,elmoznino2024,elmoznino2026}.

\paragraph{Tests outside the spectrum.} Two tests sit outside the spectrum, because every injective system passes them. Unrestricted probes and recodings fix no class, and readouts scored on training parts hold out nothing.

\paragraph{A claim should name its rung.} A claim that a system is compositional should say which rung it reaches and declare the terms of that rung. A claim of weak compositionality scores the system's own procedure on new combinations, a claim of strong compositionality states its readout class, part codes and held-out parts, and a claim of shared role codes reports the residual $\delta$.

\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:experiments}

In this section, we state the hypotheses we test, the systems we test them on, and the protocol and metrics that score each system's behavior and its states on the same held-out parts.

\subsection{Hypotheses}

Each hypothesis follows from the definitions and is tested on parts held out from every fitted procedure.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{H1. The state bounds behavior.} Where a system's own procedure acts correctly on held-out parts, a readout of the state it reads recovers those parts (Proposition~\ref{prop:subset}).
  \item \textbf{H2. Behavior can lag the state.} A system can act with the weak form while its states decompose, so its procedures fail on held-out parts that a readout of its state recovers.
  \item \textbf{H3. Reading the state lifts behavior.} A procedure that reads a recoverable state reaches the strong capabilities of Table~\ref{tab:capabilities} that the system's own procedure lacks.
  \item \textbf{H4. Recoverability has a source.} A state's recoverability comes from its input, its architecture or its training, and comparing the three locates it.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Systems}

Systems trained from scratch let us hold parts out from the system itself and score its own procedures on them. Pretrained models saw their parts in pretraining, so we hold parts out from the readout alone and use random weights, training checkpoints and invented words as controls (Table~\ref{tab:systems}).

\paragraph{Glyph networks.} A fixed random generator renders a latent $z \in \R^{8}$ as an $8 \times 8$ glyph, and a composite places two glyphs on an $8 \times 16$ canvas under one of three relations. \textsc{beside} uses disjoint regions, \textsc{over} draws both glyphs in one region with the second mirrored, and \textsc{occlude} draws the second at half scale over the first, a non-additive relation. An encoder writes a state $h \in \R^m$, and a decoder receives $h$ and a target relation and renders the same parts in the same roles under that relation. The state is the system's representation, and the decoder is its own procedure. We train an MLP, a CNN, a transformer and a role--filler decoder that decodes two halves of $h$ with one shared glyph decoder, at widths $m$ from 8 to 64 and vocabularies $V$ from 8 to 64 glyphs, with 9 seeds. With $V = 8$ glyphs, a network trains on 34 ordered pairs until its training loss nears zero. Eight new pairs of the same glyphs test weak compositionality, and 256 composites of glyphs outside its training test strong compositionality.

\paragraph{Recursive composers.} Balanced binary trees of depth $n$ have Gaussian leaves in $\R^8$. A recursive auto-associative memory \citep{pollack1990} applies one learned composer at every node and one learned decomposer recursively, and we train both on depths 1 and 2. The decomposer is the system's own procedure, and a population readout maps the same root state to all $2^n$ leaves. Holographic reduced representations (HRR) of the same width $w$ use one pair of unitary role vectors per level, with 5 seeds for each $w$ from 16 to 256.

\paragraph{Pretrained models.} Sentences place two nouns in the roles of five asymmetric relations, such as ``a $x$ on a $y$'', and of the control ``a $x$ and a $y$''. We split 305 concrete nouns into 165 for fitting readouts, 40 for validation and 100 held out. Photographs come from Tiny-ImageNet \citep{le2015tiny}, whose 200 classes we split into 120 for fitting, 30 for validation and 50 held out, and two photographs share a $224 \times 224$ canvas \textsc{beside} or \textsc{above} each other, or \textsc{overlap}ping. We test eleven text encoders from CLIP \citep{radford2021clip}, including those that condition Stable Diffusion \citep{rombach2022}, T5 \citep{raffel2020t5}, BERT \citep{devlin2019bert} and GPT-2 \citep{radford2019gpt2}. The language models are Qwen3-8B \citep{yang2025qwen3}, OLMo-2 7B and 13B \citep{olmo2024olmo2}, phi-4 \citep{abdin2024phi4}, and seven Pythia models from 160 million to 12 billion parameters \citep{biderman2023pythia}, with ten checkpoints of Pythia-1.4b and nine each of Pythia-410m and five PolyPythia reruns \citep{vanderwal2025polypythias}. The image encoders are CLIP ViT-B/32 and ViT-H/14 \citep{radford2021clip,cherti2023}, DINOv2 small, large and giant \citep{oquab2024dinov2}, and SigLIP so400m \citep{zhai2023siglip}.

\paragraph{Roles by meaning and behavior.} Each relation has a \emph{converse} paraphrase that reverses the order of mention and keeps the meaning, such as ``a tulip on a barrel'' and ``a barrel under a tulip'' (Figure~\ref{fig:protocol}c). The tulip is the \emph{figure} in both, and the barrel is the \emph{ground}. The language models answer questions such as ``Which object is on top?'' after four demonstrations with fitting nouns. A test prompt ends with ``Sentence: a tulip on a barrel. Question: Which object is on top? Answer: the'', and the model's state at that final token is the \emph{question state}. Image--text encoders match a composite against a caption that names its objects in their places and against the caption with the places swapped, and text encoders match a sentence against its converse paraphrase and against the same paraphrase with the nouns exchanged.

\subsection{Protocol}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_protocol.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{One protocol reads the same held-out parts through three procedures.} \textbf{a.} We split the parts before fitting anything (1). The system encodes composites of two parts (2, 3), and affine readouts fitted on the fitting pool return each role's part from the state, with error $\varepsilon$ on held-out parts (4). The same steps, run on the concatenation and on the sum of the same part codes, calibrate $\varepsilon$ into the score $\kappa$ (5). \textbf{b.} A population readout measures the representation, a vocabulary readout stands in for a typical probe, and the system's own procedure measures behavior. \textbf{c.} A converse paraphrase keeps the figure and reverses the order of mention, so a readout of the figure fitted on one phrasing returns the tulip on the other if roles follow meaning and the barrel if they follow word order. Colors keep one meaning in every figure. Orange marks the system's own procedure and blue a declared readout of its state. Green marks a procedure that reads through the state, such as a new decoder, an edit or a patch, grey marks controls and references, and red marks a blend or an answer by word order. Filled markers denote held-out parts and open markers training parts.}
  \label{fig:protocol}

We split the parts before fitting anything. Readouts and role maps fit composites of a fitting pool, validation parts choose ridge penalties, layers and part codes, and held-out parts give every reported number. For a pretrained model, a part code is the model's state for one part alone (``a $x$'', ``there is a $x$ here'', or the photograph), reduced to 16 principal components and whitened \citep{timkey2021}. For glyph networks, we declare the top 16 principal components of glyph pixels as the part code. Each system also runs with random weights, which keep its architecture, tokenizer and input format and remove learning. Every number is a mean over seeds, and Appendix~\ref{app:protocol} gives further details.

\subsection{Metrics}

Every metric scores composites of held-out parts.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Recovery $R^2$.} For a procedure $u$ that returns a part code, $R^2 = 1 - \varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon$ as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:recover}, so 0 predicts the mean and 1 recovers the part exactly. It scores readouts, decoders and decomposers alike.
  \item \textbf{Recoverability $\kappa$.} Eq.~\eqref{eq:kappa} rescales a readout's error so that the sum of the part codes scores 0 and their concatenation scores 1.
  \item \textbf{Transfer.} A readout of the figure fits canonical sentences and reads converse ones. Transfer is the share of converse sentences whose prediction $\hat{e}$ lies nearer the figure's code than the ground's, $\lVert \hat{e} - e(\text{figure}) \rVert < \lVert \hat{e} - e(\text{ground}) \rVert$, so roles by meaning score 1 and word order scores 0.
  \item \textbf{Answer accuracy.} The share of questions on which the model prefers the correct noun by summed log-probability. Half of the questions ask for the figure and half for the ground, in both phrasings, so an answer by word order scores 0.5.
  \item \textbf{Flip rate.} The share of held-out pairs whose answer changes to the source's answer when we patch the question state, or one subspace of it, from a source prompt with the opposite figure.
  \item \textbf{Role residual $\delta$.} The share of a state's variance left after the role maps of Eq.~\eqref{eq:factor}, so 0 means one shared code per role.
\end{itemize}

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

In this section, we test the four hypotheses. Behavior on held-out parts never exceeds the state outside a limit of our readouts (H1), it often lags the state (H2), procedures that read the state close the gap (H3), and pretrained models build their recoverability while small networks inherit it (H4). Figure~\ref{fig:ladder} summarizes the first three.

\subsection{H1. The state bounds behavior}
\label{sec:behavior}

\textbf{Models that answer by meaning carry the role in the question state.} Asked which object is on top in ``a cake on a plate'' and in its converse ``a plate under a cake'', phi-4 answers \emph{cake} both times, and a readout of its question state also returns \emph{cake} both times (Figure~\ref{fig:both}a). Across held-out nouns and five relations, Qwen3-8B and phi-4 answer with accuracy 0.99, OLMo-2 13B with 0.92 and OLMo-2 7B with 0.77, in either word order. Their question states transfer at 0.98 in phi-4, 0.87 in Qwen3-8B and 0.73 in OLMo-2 13B, in the order of their answer accuracy. Across four prompts and seven models, transfer correlates with answer accuracy at $r = 0.94$, with a 95\% interval of 0.82--0.99 over models (Figure~\ref{fig:both}b). Within a single model the correlation is 0.88, so a prompt that changes a model's answers also changes its transfer.

\textbf{Patching the question state moves the answer.} Replacing the final-token state with that of a source with the opposite figure flips the answer on 0.99 of held-out pairs in phi-4, 0.84 in Qwen3-8B and 0.80 in OLMo-2 13B (Figure~\ref{fig:both}c). A source with the same figure leaves the answer in place on 0.78--1.00. The same patch in the first half of the layers flips at most 0.21, so the answer reaches this token late.

\textbf{A low-dimensional subspace carries the answer.} Patching the readout's 16 dimensions changes the noun it returns and flips at most 0.06 of answers, and patching everything except those dimensions flips 0.67--0.98 (Figure~\ref{fig:both}d). The top 64 directions of the difference between source and target, fitted on other nouns, flip 0.76, 0.75 and 0.51, against at most 0.03 for 64 random directions. A subspace that generalizes to held-out nouns therefore carries the answer, and the 16-dimensional readout recovers a redundant part of it.

\textbf{Answers reach parts held out from the model itself.} On invented words such as \emph{blicket}, models that answer by meaning keep accuracy at 1.00 in phi-4, 0.98 in Qwen3-8B, 0.79 in OLMo-2 13B and 0.71 in OLMo-2 7B. Transfer falls to 0.75, 0.66, 0.53 and 0.41 in the same order, and an invented word takes about 2.5 tokens against 1.1 for a real noun.

\textbf{Models that answer by word order fail in both places.} Pythia-1.4b answers \emph{plate} for ``a cake on a plate'' and \emph{cake} for ``a plate under a cake'', the last-mentioned noun each time, and a readout of its question state returns the first-mentioned noun each time (Figure~\ref{fig:weak}b). Every Pythia and GPT-2 model answers questions about roles at 0.41--0.56, from 160 million to 12 billion parameters and at every checkpoint of Pythia-1.4b, and their question states transfer at 0.07--0.10.

\textbf{Sentence states follow word order in every model.} A readout of the figure fitted on canonical sentences transfers to converse sentences at 0.00--0.05 from the token-averaged state of every language model, against 0.15 at initialization, so training strengthens the binding to word order (Figure~\ref{fig:weak}c). Kernel and two-layer perceptron readouts of the token-averaged and noun states of phi-4, Qwen3-8B and OLMo-2 13B transfer at 0.00--0.06. Fitted on both phrasings, the readout assigns the figure with accuracy 0.79--0.85, so the trained states carry the figure through codes that change with the phrasing and violate role-factorization for semantic roles. Text encoders fail the same way. Eight of them tell a sentence's nouns from others with accuracy 0.89--1.00, yet they match a sentence to its converse paraphrase over the same paraphrase with the nouns exchanged with accuracy 0.02--0.15 (Figure~\ref{fig:weak}d).

\textbf{OLMo-2 7B marks a limit of the declared readouts.} OLMo-2 7B answers by meaning on 0.77 of held-out pairs, while a ridge readout of its question state transfers at only 0.20 (Figure~\ref{fig:weak}a). Whole-state patches flip 0.37 of its answers at layer 16, where we fit the readout, and 0.77 at layer 24 and 0.85 at layer 32. Ridge, kernel and MLP readouts at any of these layers transfer at 0.51 or below, and at 0.56 with hyperparameters chosen on the test set. The state carries the role, and our readout classes fail to reach it. A class containing the model's own later layers would place OLMo-2 7B on the diagonal of Figure~\ref{fig:ladder}, so this case leaves H1 intact and shows that recoverability depends on the declared class.

\subsection{H2. Behavior can lag the state}
\label{sec:states}

  \centering

\textbf{Glyph networks are weakly compositional in behavior.} Every architecture reproduces new pairs of training glyphs with output $R^2$ between 0.85 and 1.00, except the transformer trained on eight glyphs. Held-out glyphs separate them. At $m = 64$, their output rises from 0.20--0.34 at $V = 8$ to 0.85--0.91 at $V = 64$ (Figure~\ref{fig:strong}b).

\textbf{Their states decompose held-out glyphs.} Trained on 8 glyphs, one MLP renders five held-out composites with $R^2 = -0.15$, while an affine readout of its state recovers them with 0.76 (Figure~\ref{fig:strong}a). Across the MLP, CNN and role--filler networks, a population readout recovers 0.83--0.86 at $V = 8$ and 0.89 at $V = 64$, which gives $\kappa = 0.73$ and $0.83$. The glyph code is informative, because an affine map recovers the latent with $R^2 = 0.92$. The transformer is the exception, with 0.59 at $V = 8$.

\textbf{The decoders behave like readouts fitted on their vocabulary.} A vocabulary readout of the state recovers only 0.23--0.29 at $V = 8$, and it tracks each network's own output better than a population readout does, as Proposition~\ref{prop:span} predicts. Distinct parts drive the decoder's transfer. With 140 training pairs, moving from 16 to 64 glyphs raises the output on held-out glyphs from 0.47--0.64 to 0.81--0.82.

\textbf{Recursion shows the same gap at depth.} Within its training depths, a recursive composer's own decomposer recovers held-out leaves almost perfectly for $w \ge 32$. At depth 3 it falls to $R^2 = 0.26$ at $w = 256$, while a population readout recovers the same leaves with 0.99 (Figure~\ref{fig:strong}c). At depths 4 and 5, which the composer never saw, the readout stays within 0.14 of the ceiling of Proposition~\ref{prop:depth}. Recursive unbinding of HRR loses about $(2^n - 1)d/w$ of the variance to crosstalk, falling at $w = 256$ from 0.96 at depth 1 to $-0.13$ at depth 5, while a population readout of the same HRR recovers its leaves exactly whenever $w \ge 2^n d$.

\textbf{Image encoders hold places that their matching ignores.} CLIP-B/32, CLIP-H/14 and SigLIP prefer a caption that names the two held-out photographs of a composite over a caption with one wrong object, with accuracy 0.88--0.97, and over the caption with the two places swapped at chance, 0.50--0.51 (Figure~\ref{fig:strong}d). Their quadrant states recover both photographs in their places with $\kappa = 0.81$--$0.94$, and under \textsc{overlap} with 0.85--0.94 against 0.29--0.72 for random weights.

\subsection{H3. Reading the state lifts behavior}
\label{sec:success}

Each failure of the previous subsection has a repair that reads through the state (Table~\ref{tab:remedies}). The tests follow the strong capabilities of Table~\ref{tab:capabilities}. A new decoder tests held-out parts, written codes test new parts in every role and additions without forgetting, training at depth tests nesting, and edits of role codes test inspection.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_decomp.pdf}

The natural direction $h(a', b') - h(a, b)$, scaled to the same norm, lands far from the target. Numbers give $R^2$ to the target. \textbf{b.} Writing a new glyph's code through $B_2$ keeps old pairs (dark) and renders the new glyph in a role it never filled (light), with no gradient step, where fine-tuning damages both and replay needs stored data. \textbf{c.} A role-1 edit changes the role-2 half of the output less than other changes of the state. \textbf{d.} In language models that answer by meaning, the state at two role questions places a sentence's converse paraphrase nearer than both role reversals, with no fitted readout, where the token-averaged state never does; chance is $1/3$ (dotted). Means over 3--5 seeds, with standard errors.}
  \label{fig:decomp}

\textbf{A new decoder reaches held-out parts.} We freeze the encoder of each glyph network trained on 8 glyphs and train a new decoder of the same architecture on composites of 64 other glyphs. Its output on held-out glyphs rises from 0.25 to 0.75, against 0.88 for networks trained end to end on 64 glyphs. Across architectures and vocabularies, the population readout of a frozen encoder correlates with the output of a new decoder at $r = 0.87$, so recoverability predicts the procedures a state can support.

\textbf{Edits of role codes act on one part.} Under \textsc{over}, the residual $\delta$ is 0.02--0.04 for the MLP, CNN and role--filler networks and 0.06--0.13 for the transformer, so the states reach recoverability through shared role codes. The decoder renders reversals predicted by the role maps as well as true ones, 0.86 against 0.86 at $V = 64$. An interchange edit renders the donor glyph with $R^2 = 0.78$, against 0.81 when the network sees the donor itself (Figure~\ref{fig:decomp}a), and it spills 0.16 into the untouched half of the output, against 0.90 for the natural direction $h(a', b') - h(a, b)$ (Figure~\ref{fig:decomp}c). The decoders therefore use the role codes, which answers the objection of \citet{fodor1990} for these systems. Under the non-additive \textsc{occlude}, $\delta$ rises to 0.11--0.22 and edits spill 0.31.

\textbf{Written codes add parts without forgetting.} Fine-tuned on a held-out glyph in role~1 alone, networks trained on 64 glyphs render it in role~2 worse than before, 0.62 against 0.75, and their new pairs fall from 0.98 to 0.90 (Figure~\ref{fig:decomp}b). The damage accumulates, as in catastrophic interference \citep{mccloskey1989}, and a network trained on 16 glyphs and fine-tuned on 32 more in sequence drops old pairs to 0.18. Writing a new glyph's code into role~2 through $B_2$, with no gradient step, renders it at 0.75 with no cost to old pairs. A network that adds 32 glyphs in sequence this way keeps old pairs at 0.99 and renders each new glyph in a role it never filled at 0.71. Replay does better on new glyphs, by 0.11--0.21, at the cost of storing old data and taking gradient steps.

\textbf{Training at depth repairs the decomposer.} A decomposer that trains with its composer on trees of depth 3 to 5 recovers held-out leaves at 1.00, 1.00 and 0.97 at width 256, the values of the population readout, and at width 64 it meets the bound of Proposition~\ref{prop:depth}. Trained alone on the composer's states, the decomposer reaches 0.74, 0.29 and 0.09, and no condition transfers to depth 6.

\textbf{Single parts teach the decoder to render parts.} Networks trained on all pairs of 8 glyphs and on 64 other glyphs shown alone, each in one role with the other role blank, render a held-out glyph alone with $R^2 = 0.88$, against $-0.30$ without them, and held-out pairs with 0.47, against 0.26.

\textbf{The question state serves as an embedding.} A sentence embedding read at the end of two questions, one asking for the figure and one for the ground, places the converse paraphrase nearer than both role reversals for 100\% of held-out pairs in phi-4, 92\% in Qwen3-8B and 83\% in OLMo-2 13B, with no fitted readout (Figure~\ref{fig:decomp}d). The same embedding fails in Pythia-1.4b and GPT-2-large, whose answers follow word order.

\subsection{H4. Where recoverability comes from}
\label{sec:origins}

We compare each state with its input and with the same architecture under random weights (Figure~\ref{fig:origins}). For the glyph networks, two further conditions test the input's role. \emph{Entangled inputs} pass each composite through a fixed random map before the encoder, three rounds of an orthogonal rotation and $\tanh$ at gain 4, and \emph{recovery objectives} add the linear head described above.

\textbf{Glyph networks inherit recoverability from their input.} The input pixels reach 0.91 and untrained networks 0.82--0.83, and a frozen untrained encoder supports an equally good new decoder (0.75), so training in these small networks mostly shapes the decoder (Figure~\ref{fig:origins}a). A fixed random map that entangles the input lowers its own readout to 0.68. The MLP, CNN and role--filler networks then read at 0.54--0.55, which is 0.06--0.08 above untrained encoders and 0.13--0.15 below the input, and the transformer reads at 0.28, level with its untrained encoder. No condition we tried lifts a state above its input. The glyph networks therefore establish the gap between a state and its decoder, and the recoverability they keep belongs to the input. Even so, recovery predicts the output of a new decoder, with $r = 0.98$ on entangled inputs.

\textbf{Pretrained models build recoverability beyond their input.} Averaging a sentence's input embeddings gives $\kappa = -0.21$ to $-0.49$ across seven language models, below the sum. Concatenating the two nouns' input embeddings, which grants the location of each noun, gives $-0.12$ to 0.49, and token-averaged states exceed it in five of the seven models, with GPT-2 large and Pythia-1.4b level with it (Figure~\ref{fig:origins}b). For photographs, raw pixels give $\kappa = -0.61$ to $-0.69$ against each encoder's own part codes at the layer of its quadrant state, far below quadrant states at 0.81--0.94 and below random weights at 0.29--0.72 (Figure~\ref{fig:origins}c).

\textbf{Training raises recoverability, and scale does not.} The pipeline recovers the concatenation reference with $R^2 = 1.00$ and the sum reference with 0.48. Among the text encoders, the GPT-2 models reach $\kappa = 0.44$--$0.50$, CLIP-H 0.36 and the others 0.06--0.26, against $-0.11$ to $0.15$ under random weights (Figure~\ref{fig:origins}d). Within Pythia, $\kappa$ rises from 0.27 at 160 million parameters to 0.56--0.58 at 6.9 and 12 billion, and Qwen3-8B, OLMo-2 and phi-4 reach 0.40--0.49 (Figure~\ref{fig:origins}e). Pythia-1.4b peaks at 0.71 after 1{,}000 steps, 0.7\% of training, and declines to 0.47, and six runs of Pythia-410m peak at about the same point (Figure~\ref{fig:origins}f).

\textbf{Word order and architecture supply part of it.} Causal attention keeps the tokens of the first noun from seeing the second, so in GPT-2 and CLIP random weights already recover the first-mentioned noun with $\kappa = 0.50$--$0.58$. The control ``a $x$ and a $y$'' gives the same $\kappa$ as the asymmetric relations in every encoder, so the readouts recover the order of the nouns. Trained image encoders reach $\kappa = 0.17$--$0.49$ in their token-averaged states and 0.81--0.94 in their quadrant states, so the patch states keep two photographs apart and averaging over patches blends them.

\textbf{Width and relation set ceilings.} At $m = 8$ glyph states reach 0.67--0.71 against the Eckart--Young ceiling of 0.79 (Proposition~\ref{prop:ey}), and the non-additive \textsc{occlude} relation lowers recovery to 0.75--0.83.

\textbf{A random hierarchy supplies none of it.} In the random hierarchy model \citep{cagnetta2024rhm}, random rule tables expand a root symbol through levels of latent symbols into a string of tokens. We score affine readouts of the one-hot string against codes of the latents that the grammar alone fixes (Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}). Across 144 settings of vocabulary, rules per symbol, arity, depth, level and part code, the input recovers the latents of held-out symbols with $R^2$ between $-0.63$ and $-0.03$, and the latents of training symbols with up to 0.90. On this grammar, any recoverability of a trained network's state must come from training, which makes it the next test of whether training builds recoverable states.

\section{Conclusion and limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

We described compositionality as a spectrum of capabilities with two forms. Weak compositionality recombines known parts, and strong compositionality keeps every weak capability and adds decomposition, the return of each part in its role from a whole. Decomposition becomes a useful notion only under a simple class of procedures and on held-out parts, and recoverability scores it under both conditions. These terms gave a shared language that placed fifteen accounts of compositionality on one spectrum.

The evidence supports the hypothesis that behavior is a subset of representation. Language models that answered by meaning carried the role in the state at the question, and patches of that state changed their answers. Glyph networks, recursive composers and image encoders kept parts in their states that their own procedures lost, and procedures that read those states reached the strong capabilities the systems lacked. OLMo-2 7B answered beyond our readouts, a limit of the declared class, and models that answered by word order failed in both places. Pretrained models built recoverability beyond their inputs, and small trained networks inherited it from theirs.

Our results suggest five requirements for a strongly compositional network. Its state must have room for every part (Propositions~\ref{prop:ceiling} and~\ref{prop:ey}). Its procedure for reading parts must learn from many distinct parts (Proposition~\ref{prop:span}). Each part should enter every role through one code, so that one composite fixes its reversal (Proposition~\ref{prop:systematic}). The role components must be the ones the system uses. The relation must stay invertible on its own outputs, and the system's procedure must follow it (Proposition~\ref{prop:depth}). A claim that a model is compositional should name its form and its rung, and declare the readout class, part codes and held-out parts behind it.

\paragraph{Limitations and next steps.} The inclusion of behavior in representation is a logical result for a readout class that contains the system's procedure, and the evidence that it is strict comes from the systems we tested. Our evidence for design comes from repairs and from a recovery objective that also adds data, and separating the objective from the data is the next test. Our trained networks are small and their domains rendered, chosen so that we could hold out parts, and their recoverability comes largely from their inputs. In pretrained models the parts are held out only from the readout, apart from invented words. Richer methods, such as a discovered tensor-product decomposition \citep{mccoy2019}, may find structure our readouts miss. A human study could ask whether people show the same gap, by testing whether they recover the parts of composites built from newly learned parts.
