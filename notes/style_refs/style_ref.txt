ABSTRACT
Compositional structure is pervasive in nature and across scales: atoms combine into molecules,
molecules into cells, and cells into organisms, while further compositional hierarchies appear within
each level. More broadly, compositionality — the reuse and recombination of parts into novel
structures — is a fundamental source of interesting complexity in both natural and artificial systems.
Yet despite its importance, and despite our apparent ease in recognizing it, mathematical accounts
capturing what we intuitively mean by compositional structure remain limited. Here, we fill this gap
by formalizing a simple idea: compositional structure emerges when parts, considered together, reveal
shared structure that is not captured when those parts are modeled separately. We express this idea
using algorithmic information theory — the mathematics of information and compression — which
provides a natural way to identify structure in arbitrary data without specifying in advance what that
structure must look like. Applied across scales, our definition informs where and how compositional
structure emerges within an object. Using tractable approximations, we then invert the framework to
generate compositional objects: we specify the desired form of their compositional structure without
prescribing their particular content, and optimize directly over strings and images. The resulting
objects exhibit readily recognizable hierarchical reuse and part–whole organization, suggesting that
the definition captures compositional structure rather than merely complexity, regularity, or shared
information. Finally, we discuss implications for machine learning, including a new perspective on
depth in neural architectures and principles for constructing compositional data curricula capable of
supporting open-ended learning.
Keywords compositionality, complexity, structure, compression, machine learning

“Each portion of matter can be conceived as a garden full of plants, and as a pond full of fish. But each branch of the

— Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, (Leibniz, 1898)
Compositional structure is everywhere, and at every scale. Atoms combine into molecules, molecules into cells, cells
into organs, and organs into organisms. Each of these levels in turn contains further rich hierarchies; cells and organs, too,
are intricately organized assemblies of interacting parts. Compositionality applies to more abstract spaces as well: music
develops motifs into phrases and movements; programs build modules from modules and functions from functions;
concepts are built from other concepts; technologies are built from more granular technologies (Arthur, 2009); scientific
theories inherit and reorganize earlier ideas; and a well-designed curriculum continually recombines prior knowledge
into more sophisticated skills. Across all these examples, complexity does not arise from an undifferentiated collection
of elements, but from parts being reused and recombined into new structures. In this broad sense, compositionality
is a signature of both life and intelligence, and arguably one of the principal sources of interesting complexity in the
universe (Agüera y Arcas, 2025).

Despite how readily we recognize such examples, it remains difficult to say precisely what makes them compositional.
Familiar descriptions — that the whole is more than the sum of its parts, or that the meaning of a complex expression
depends on its structure and the meanings of its constituents — are suggestive but do not by themselves provide a
general quantitative measure (Pagin and Westerståhl, 2010). More formal approaches in machine learning typically
begin with a prescribed set of semantic constituents and a rule for reconstructing the whole from them. Some define
these constituents using known generative factors or object slots (Wiedemer et al., 2024, 2023), while others restrict
their combination to a particular operation, such as a linear map or a prescribed hierarchy (Andreas, 2019; Trager et al.,
2023). These approaches are useful when the parts and their modes of combination are already known, but they make
compositionality relative to externally supplied semantics or a restricted form of composition (Elmoznino et al., 2024).
They therefore do not fully answer the broader question: for an arbitrary object, what intrinsic compositional structure
emerges across its parts, how much of it is present, and how is it organized across scales?
Our starting point is that compositional structure emerges when parts, considered together, reveal shared structure
that is not captured when those parts are modeled separately. Here, a model represents the structure in an object: the
regularities and patterns that provide a shorter explanation of the data. If the model of the whole contains something
genuinely new beyond the models of the parts, then those parts share some pattern or organization that becomes apparent
only through their combination. This is what we call novel compositional structure. A cell viewed in isolation reveals
its internal organization; a collection of cells viewed together may additionally reveal a tissue, with spatial arrangements
and collective mechanisms absent from any one cell. The same principle can be applied recursively: tissues form organs,
motifs form phrases, and functions form higher-level functions. Repeating the comparison at different locations and
scales produces a profile of where an object’s compositional structure emerges. Section 2 develops this idea and tests it
against several intuitive cases.
To make this precise, we use algorithmic information theory: a mathematics of information expressed through
description length and compression. The shortest two-part description of an object separates a model, which captures its
compressible structure, from the residual information needed to specify the object given that model. We compare these
structure-bearing models across parts and wholes, measuring how much additional information is needed to describe
the model of the whole from the models of its parts. This lets us speak rigorously about parts, structure, and novelty
without specifying in advance what the structure must be. The distinction is between form and content: our quantities
describe where and how structure is composed while remaining agnostic to the particular structures or patterns involved.
Section 3 formalizes the definition, extends it to multiscale compositional structure profiles, and develops tractable
approximations.
A quantitative definition also lets us look at the problem in reverse: instead of measuring the compositional structure of
a given object, we can specify a desired profile and search for objects that exhibit it. We demonstrate this in Section 4
by optimizing directly over strings and images. Without prescribing their particular content, the objective produces
strings with nested patterns and images with coherent, hierarchical visual organization reminiscent of abstract art. These
experiments serve both as a method for generating structured data and as a concrete test of the definition. The generated
objects display recognizable reuse, hierarchy, and part–whole organization rather than mere randomness or repetition.
Finally, we consider what this perspective suggests for machine learning. Natural data often builds structure on top
of structure across many scales, matching the inductive bias of deep architectures whose successive layers build
increasingly abstract features from earlier ones. Compositional structure can also grow through time: new data may
reuse, extend, or reorganize what came before. This suggests principles not only for choosing architectures, but for
ordering experience into curricula in which each stage builds maximally on prior structure, and for gathering new
data that continually expands what a learner can represent. We close by developing these connections between depth,
curricula, and open-ended learning to our definition of compositional structure in Section 5.

Intuition for compositional structure
Before introducing the formal machinery, we build intuition through a running concrete example based on computer
programs. We use computer programs because they are familiar and paradigmatic compositional objects: programmers
explicitly define reusable parts and combine them to construct more complex ones, making the relevant structure
unusually easy to see. A self-contained program can be separated conceptually into a library of reusable functions,
classes, and data structures, and the remaining code written using that library. When the library is designed to make the
complete program as short as possible, it captures the program’s reusable structure. A richly compositional program
should then have a modular library containing a varied repertoire of components that are reused throughout the program
and recombined to define higher-level components. A library with very few distinct components provides little material
to compose. Conversely, a large collection of functions used only within separate portions of the code is better
understood as several unrelated programs placed beside one another; size alone does not make a library compositional.

The same program can be organized around many different libraries, so inspecting an arbitrary implementation would
make this intuition depend on choices made by its programmer. Compression provides a less arbitrary reference
point: consider the library that yields the shortest overall description after accounting for both the library and the code
expressed through it. Our strategy is to track how this optimal library changes as increasingly large parts of the program
are considered together. If two snippets contain related logic, their joint description may justify a reusable abstraction
that was not economical for either snippet alone. Compositionality then depends on whether the library of the whole
contains something genuinely new: not new raw code, since the relevant code already occurred in the parts, but a new
structural description of what those parts share.
More concretely, let xa and xb denote two program fragments and let x = [xa, xb] denote their combination, with
each expressed directly as code without factoring reusable pieces into a library. Write mxa, mxb, and mx for the
optimal libraries that compress the corresponding fragments or whole. Suppose the same block of code occurs
once in each fragment and is long enough that extracting it into a function becomes worthwhile only after the
fragments are joined. The function need not appear in mxa or mxb, since defining it for a single use would not
shorten either fragment individually. For the whole, however, extracting the shared block into a function can shorten
the total program. The library mx has grown because a common part becomes reusable structure only at the level
of the whole. The same phenomenon can involve refactoring rather than simply adding a function. For example,
one fragment may justify a matrix-multiplication routine while another justifies a vector-norm routine; considered
together, the shortest library may redefine the norm in terms of the existing multiplication routine. In both cases,
combining the fragments changes how their structure is optimally represented and reveals a novel interaction between
them.
Two contrasting cases clarify why this is more specific than either reuse or structural complexity alone. First, xb
might simply provide more uses of a library that was already optimal for xa. The whole is then compressed us-
ing the same library as the parts: there is shared structure and a compression benefit, but no new abstraction
or refactoring is required.
Second, xa and xb might be entirely unrelated programs.
The optimal library of
the whole may be larger than either part-library, but only because it places their independent contents side by
side. Given mxa and mxb, constructing mx requires essentially nothing novel. Both of these cases differ from
the compositional one: in the first, all the structure needed to compress both parts is already encoded in one
of their libraries; in the second, there is no shared structure to exploit.
Compositional structure lies in a dif-
ferent space, where the best joint library cannot be obtained merely by selecting or merging the libraries of the
parts.
This comparison can be applied recursively. A program may reveal one abstraction when short fragments are
joined, another when larger segments of code are combined, and still others when those segments form the com-
plete program. Recording where the optimal library grows or is reorganized therefore describes not just whether
the program is compositional, but where its compositional structure emerges across locations and scales. The
comparison at each join is local: it asks what additional library structure emerges when those particular parts
are combined. Structure already captured within a smaller segment is therefore attributed to the earlier joins in-
side that segment, while later joins capture only the further structure introduced by combining it with other seg-
ments.
Programs and libraries provide a concrete example of a more general idea. For an arbitrary object, a model plays the
role of the library: it captures regularities that allow the object to be described more concisely, while the remaining
description specifies details not captured by the model. The object may represent music, an image, an iid dataset,
or a nonstationary data stream. In every case, we ask the same question of a chosen division into parts: does
the best model of the whole contain structural information that cannot be recovered from the best models of the
parts? The next section makes “best model” and “cannot be recovered” precise using algorithmic information theory.
Importantly, the claim is not that the raw whole contains information absent from its parts: by construction, it is
simply their concatenation. Rather, combining the parts can change the best account of their structure. It is in
this precise sense that the description of a compositional whole is more than the sum of the descriptions of its
parts.
3
Defining compositional structure
The preceding intuition suggests that compositional structure can be identified by comparing the structure-bearing
descriptions of parts and wholes. We now make this idea precise using algorithmic information theory. We first use
optimal two-part codes (Grünwald, 2007) to define the model that captures an object’s compressible structure; we then
measure the novel structure that emerges at a composition step, apply this comparison recursively to obtain multiscale
compositional structure profiles, and describe how the resulting quantities can be approximated in practice.

3.1
Preliminaries: algorithmic information theory
Kolmogorov complexity.
Kolmogorov complexity (Fortnow, 2000; Kolmogorov, 1965; Li et al., 2008) is a notion of
information quantity that serves as the foundations for algorithmic information theory. Intuitively, the Kolmogorov
complexity of an object x, denoted K(x), is the length of the shortest program (in some programming language) that
outputs x. A related notion is the conditional Kolmogorov complexity of x given another object y, denoted K(x | y),
which is the length of the shortest program that takes y as input and outputs x. Kolmogorov complexity has many
intuitive properties as a measure of information quantity that generalize equivalent properties from Shannon information
theory (Grünwald and Vitányi, 2003), and is deeply rooted in the idea of compression.
Formally, throughout this paper K denotes prefix Kolmogorov complexity relative to a fixed universal prefix Turing
machine U. A prefix Turing machine is one in which valid programs are self-delimiting; they are commonly-used in
algorithmic information theory for technical convenience. For a finite string x, K(x) is the length l(r) (in bits) of the
shortest binary program r that prints x and halts:
K(x) = min
r {l(r) : U(r) = x, r ∈{0, 1}∗},
(1)
and given a separate string y, the conditional Kolmogorov complexity K(x | y) is defined analogously as:

For tuples, let ⟨x, y⟩denote a fixed computable self-delimiting encoding of x and y, and use K(x, y) as shorthand for
K(⟨x, y⟩); conditioning on several objects is interpreted similarly. The particular choices of universal machine and
tuple encoding affect these quantities by at most an additive constant independent of the objects. In general, K(·) is
incomputable. However, the quantity can still be useful and approximated.
Note 1. Suppression of lower-order terms
Kolmogorov-complexity relationships generally hold only up to small additive terms. Changing the universal
machine or a fixed encoding contributes an O(1) constant, while recording lengths, boundaries, or other auxiliary
information can contribute O(log n) bits for objects of length n. Throughout the paper, we suppress these terms
and write the corresponding equalities and inequalities directly. This convention keeps the description-length
arguments transparent without changing their qualitative content. Accordingly, statements involving zeros,
strict inequalities, or exact ties should be understood up to these suppressed terms: the relevant differences are
assumed to be large enough that coding overheads cannot change the comparison.
Structure and Occam’s razor.
Although Kolmogorov complexity provides a canonical notion of information quantity,
it does not separate structural complexity from randomness. A regular string has low complexity because it can be
generated by a short program, whereas a random string has high complexity because no substantially shorter description
exists than the string itself. However, the latter form of complexity does not reflect elaborate internal organization; it
reflects the absence of compressible structure. In this sense, K(x) entangles two distinct contributions to description
length: one arising from meaningful structure, and one arising from irreducible randomness. This motivates finer
notions that aim to measure structure rather than mere incompressibility.
This distinction between structural and random information was important to Kolmogorov himself, who quantified
it through a new metric (Kolmogorov, 1973) that was later generalized under various formulations and termed
sophistication or meaningful information (Gács et al., 2001; Grünwald et al., 2008; Koppel and Atlan, 1989; Vitányi,
2006). Intuitively, measuring structural information involves compressing an object using two-part coding schemes that
follow the minimum description length (MDL) principle (Grünwald, 2007) and Occam’s razor.
Let M be a model class, and denote the two-part code length of x under a model m ∈M as:
Lm(x) = K(m) + lm(x),
(3)
where lm(x) is the model-to-data code length (i.e., the cost of describing the data given the model). The form of lm(x)
depends on the model class under consideration. For instance, in the case of computable probability distributions,
lm(x) = −log2 m(x), where m(x) is the probability mass that m assigns to x.

3.2
Formal definition
Novel compositional structure.
Using the notion of an Occam’s razor model defined in Equation (5), we provide our
formal definition for novel compositional structure.
Definition 1. Novel compositional structure
Let x = [xa, xb] denote a decomposition of a string x into two substrings xa and xb, where [xa, xb] denotes
concatenation. Let m· denote an Occam’s razor model as defined in Equation (5), and assume that it is uniquely
defined for each of x, xa, and xb. We define the amount of novel compositional structure in x relative to this
decomposition as:
C(xa, xb →x) := K(mx | mxa, mxb).
(6)
This quantity measures the additional information required to describe the model of the whole string, mx, given the
models of the parts, mxa and mxb. In particular, C(xa, xb →x) ≈0 in cases where the structure of the whole is
already recoverable from the structures of the parts. For example, this holds when mx is identical to one of mxa or
mxb, or when mx is a trivial combination of the pair (mxa, mxb) with only minor overhead (e.g., mx compresses x by
using mxa to compress xa and using mxb to compress xb).
Thus, C(xa, xb →x) is intended to quantify structure present in the whole that is not recoverable from the models of
the parts alone. Intuitively, C(xa, xb →x) is large when jointly considering xa and xb reveals a novel compression
strategy that does not arise when they are modeled separately, such as when a pattern repeats across the boundary
between xa and xb but not within either substring individually. More broadly, it measures the extent to which the
structure of the whole is different from the structures of the parts.
Note that Definition 1 measures only novel compositional structure from the decomposition of x into xa and xb; it does
not quantify the compositional structure that is already present in the parts themselves.
Compositional structure profiles.
The quantity C(xa, xb →x) measures novel compositional structure relative to a
single decomposition of a string. More generally, if x = [x1, . . . , xk], we define:
C(x1, . . . , xk →x) := K(mx | mx1, . . . , mxk).

An image dataset makes the connection between hierarchical compositional structure and scale concrete. A single
image of a cat contains many edge segments, textures, and simple shapes, so these recurring structures may already
justify their place in its optimal model; with only one cat, however, introducing a reusable concept of a cat may not yet
repay its description length cost. Across many images, cats recur often enough for the joint model to represent them as
compositions of lower-level structures; with still more data, rarer regularities can justify further distinctions such as
breeds or recurring relations between cats and indoor or outdoor scenes. Larger datasets therefore expose progressively
higher levels of the hierarchy, since rarer combinations involving more parts require more observations before they
improve compression. The compositional structure profile records the scales at which these new abstractions become
worthwhile, and for non-stationary data additionally records the locations.
The choice of decomposition.
Up to this point, Definition 2 has defined a compositional structure profile in a way
that depends on the choice of decomposition tree T. However, intuitions about compositionality strongly suggest that
a given object has an intrinsic, or “true” decomposition. We think of a compositional object has having parts that
combine to form wholes in a particular way, and capturing those intrinsic hierarchies is an important part of defining
compositional structure. We leave this as an open problem for future work, which bears on several important questions.
For instance, if one were to use a different decomposition from this intrinsic one, would the resulting compositionality
structure profile still be meaningful? Do there exist objects which are intuitively non-compositional, but erroneously
appear as if they were under the wrong decomposition? For an object that was originally constructed through a series of
compositions, as is the case in the evolution of life (Agüera y Arcas, 2025) and technology (Arthur, 2009), would the
intrinsic decomposition recover this true history?

3.4
Relationship to other measures of compositionality
The literature on compositionality has a long history spanning many fields, so we do not aim to exhaustively survey it
here. Instead, we focus on formal definitions of compositionality that have been proposed (at times under different
names), with a particular emphasis on approaches leveraging algorithmic or Shannon information theory.
Algorithmic mutual information.
For an object x = [xa, xb], algorithmic mutual information (AMI) quantifies the
degree to which the whole is more compressible than the parts:
I(xa : xb) = K(xa) + K(xb) −K(xa, xb).
AMI has been proposed as a measure for what we here call novel compositional structure (Agüera y Arcas, 2025;
Schmidhuber, 1997), and indeed it bears many similarities to the definition we have proposed. In particular, under very
mild assumptions it can be shown that:
C(xa, xb →x) > 0 =⇒I(xa : xb) > 0,
but that the reverse does not hold (proof in Appendix C.1). Intuitively, two objects can share AMI for one of two
reasons: (i) they share existing structure in mxa and mxb that need not be redundantly encoded in mx, or (ii) they
share novel structure in mx that only appears when they are considered jointly and does not exist in either mxa or mxb
alone. C(xa, xb →x) measures only the latter case, disentangling the emergence of novel compositional structure
from simply the accumulation of more data with the same structure.
Partial information decomposition.
Partial information decomposition (PID) separates the information that two
sources Xa and Xb provide about a target Y into redundant, unique, and synergistic components (Williams and Beer,
2010):
I(Xa, Xb; Y ) = Red(Xa, Xb; Y ) + Unqa(Xa, Xb; Y )
+ Unqb(Xa, Xb; Y ) + Syn(Xa, Xb; Y ).
The synergistic term measures information about Y that is available from (Xa, Xb) jointly but from neither source
individually. It is therefore the closest Shannon-theoretic analogue of C(xa, xb →x): both notions aim to isolate
information that becomes available only when the parts are considered jointly.
The two quantities nevertheless address different settings. PID is a property of a joint distribution over sources and
a target, whereas C(xa, xb →x) is an algorithmic property of the internal structure of an individual object. This

distinction is particularly important in the intrinsic part–whole setting. If the PID target is chosen to be the whole,
Y = (Xa, Xb), then we trivially have that:
Red(Xa, Xb; (Xa, Xb)) = I(Xa; Xb),

Consequently, such a PID cannot detect structure that emerges when the sources are combined into the target. PID-based
measures of linguistic compositionality instead introduce an external target, such as a word form generated from
multiple semantic features (Socolof et al., 2022). These measures characterize the compositionality of a mapping
between variables, rather than the compositional structure intrinsic to an object.
Finally, although PID requires its components to sum to the corresponding mutual information quantities, it does
not uniquely determine how information should be allocated among the redundant, unique, and synergistic terms.
Consequently, there is no generally accepted decomposition, and existing proposals satisfy different, sometimes
incompatible, auxiliary axioms (Harder et al., 2013; Matthias et al., 2025). Our definition does not attempt a complete
information decomposition and therefore avoids selecting among these alternatives: it directly measures the conditional
description length K(mx | mxa, mxb) of the whole’s structural model, thereby quantifying structural information that
emerges only when the parts are considered jointly — an algorithmic analogue of synergistic information.
Emergence and Kolmogorov’s structure function.
Emergence is commonly understood as the appearance of
system-level properties that are novel relative to those of the system’s constituents, making it conceptually close to
compositionality. Bédard and Bergeron (2022) propose an algorithmic formalization based on Kolmogorov’s structure
function (Kolmogorov, 1973; Vereshchagin and Vitányi, 2004). Intuitively, the structure function considers increasingly
large complexity budgets for describing a model of an object and, at each budget, identifies the model m that minimizes
the remaining model-to-data codelength lm(x). A substantial drop indicates that permitting a more complex model
reveals a new explanatory strategy that shortens the overall two-part description. Their definition associates emergence
with the presence of several such drops, corresponding to multiple useful models of increasing complexity. For example,
a physical system may admit a relatively simple thermodynamic description in terms of macroscopic variables, while
larger model budgets permit more detailed descriptions involving microscopic dynamics and initial conditions.
However, the complexity of a model is not itself a scale in a decomposition of the observed object. Consequently,
successive drops need not correspond to structure within progressively larger collections of parts: they may instead
reflect different explanatory strategies for the same data. The framework therefore characterizes emergence across a
hierarchy of model complexities, but does not specifically isolate structure that emerges when constituent objects are
combined as C(xa, xb →x) does. The two approaches are thus complementary: the structure function captures the
plurality of emergent explanations available at different levels of model complexity, whereas our definition quantifies
emergent structure arising at particular composition steps.
Formal grammars and compositional semantics.
In formal semantics and programming-language theory, com-
positionality is conventionally defined relative to a specified syntax and interpretation: the meaning of a compound
expression is determined by the meanings of its constituents and the rules by which they are combined (Pagin and
Westerståhl, 2010). If f is a syntactic composition rule and M is an interpretation, this principle can be expressed as:
M(f(a, b)) = Ff(M(a), M(b)),
where Ff is the corresponding operation in the semantic domain. Category-theoretic formulations express the same
principle through structure-preserving maps, such as functors, between syntactic and semantic structures. These
frameworks characterize compositionality only once the constituents, composition rules, and semantic domain have
been specified; they do not by themselves quantify how much compositional structure an object intrinsically contains,
nor do they identify that structure from arbitrary data.
Our definition addresses these complementary questions. A grammar or functional program may itself constitute the
model mx, in which case C(xa, xb →x) measures the additional information required to describe the grammar or
library of the whole from those of its parts. In functional-programming terms, this resembles the cost of refactoring:
combining xa and xb may reveal a reusable abstraction that was not justified by either part separately. In this way,
C(xa, xb →x) is complementary to these more traditional views of compositionality: it can describe the emergence of
compositional structure in a formal grammar.
Assembly theory.
Assembly theory grew out of origins-of-life research as an attempt to differentiate living from
non-living objects (Sharma et al., 2023). It treats a living object as something that exists in large numbers of copies
and is built from simpler pieces through a sequence of allowed physical events. Once a piece has been built, it can
be reused, so the object’s assembly index is the length of its shortest construction path. Assembly theory is therefore

an MDL-based framework for characterizing living systems in which composition and hierarchy are central. In more
technical ways, however, it differs substantially from our definition of compositionality. Among other things, our
definition measures novel structure in the model of an individual object relative to the models of its parts, without
requiring a physical construction space or observed copies. It also extends to more abstract forms of composition and
distinguishes structure from random detail within an individual object.
Symbiogenesis.
Symbiogenesis is the evolutionary idea that new kinds of organisms can arise when previously
separate living systems join and become interdependent (Sagan, 1967). The familiar example is the bacterial ancestor of
mitochondria becoming part of a host cell, giving rise to eukaryotic cells. Agüera y Arcas (2025) casts this as a general
route to hierarchical complexity in which smaller replicators combine into a larger replicator, which can then combine
with others; the total structure involves describing both the parts as well as how they fit together into the larger whole.
Interestingly, in this computational account, the parts and whole carry instructions for their own reproduction — i.e.,
they are self-encoding. Our definition places no such requirement on an object’s parts or whole; it measures the novel
structure that appears when the parts are considered together, however it arose. Thus, symbiogenesis offers a plausible
mechanism for the emergence of the structure that C(xa, xb →x) measures, and for its prevalence in the natural world.

At each join in a decomposition tree, two adjacent substrings are combined into a
larger substring. We fit grammars separately to the two parts and to their combination, and use the conditional code g to
measure how much of the combined grammar cannot be recovered from the grammars fitted to the parts. Since rule
names are arbitrary, we first relabel the rules in each part grammar to find a low-cost alignment with the combined
grammar, using the nested structure of the rules to determine which ones correspond. Each rule in the combined
grammar is then either encoded directly or by describing its differences from the best-matching aligned rule in either
part grammar, whichever is shorter. Thus, carrying an existing rule from a part into the combined model costs little,
whereas a new rule that becomes economical only after the parts are combined incurs a large cost.

rules and longer rule expansions than those of the compositionality-optimized strings. This illustrates the core property
of our compositionality definition. In general, an object can be structured in multiple ways that do not require a rich
and interconnected hierarchy of patterns — this structure can be flat (as in long but low-depth grammar rules) and
independent across the object (as in grammar rules that are only used in distinct regions of the object).
We provide a more visual demonstration of these distinct forms of structure in Figure 2d, which shows “dot plots” for
the highest-reward string using each reward function, as well as for a random string. Dot plots are commonly used in
DNA analysis to identify repeated structures within a string (Gibbs and Mcintyre, 1970), and build a symmetric binary

plot as diagonal lines off of the main diagonal, making it easy to visually inspect the number, length, and locations of
these patterns. Furthermore, if a pattern contains a smaller sub-pattern that appears elsewhere in the string, it will show
up as a smaller diagonal line in the same block of rows spanned by the larger pattern (or, equivalently, block of columns
due to the matrix’s symmetry). For compositionality-optimized strings, we find that the dot plots contain a more dense
distribution of patterns and sub-patterns than those of structure-optimized or random strings. Structure-optimized
strings do still exhibit repeated patterns, but they tend to be longer, more sparse, and less hierarchical. Overall, the
dot plots of structure-optimized strings look more similar to those of random strings, but the addition of a few long
diagonals.

by training with maximum likelihood on a random half of the patch’s pixels and using the remaining half as held-out

fallback prevents the network from attributing spurious spatial structure to a patch that is better described without
spatial dependence.

left unexplained by it. Following learning-curve estimates of neural-network description length (Blier and Ollivier,
2018), we estimate the first quantity from the cumulative training loss incurred while learning the network. At each
point in training, we subtract the loss of the final selected model: the remainder measures predictive errors caused by
not yet having learned the model, rather than errors that remain even after it has been learned. Accumulating this excess
over training therefore provides a proxy for the number of bits absorbed into the network’s weights. The model-to-data
term is the selected model’s negative log-likelihood over the complete patch. Together, these terms form our empirical
two-part coding scheme f.

Reward.
Whereas a string has one spatial axis and can naturally be bisected, an image has two, so we recursively
divide both axes to obtain a quadtree decomposition. Each patch is split into four quadrants until reaching 8 × 8 patches.
The resulting joins combine 8 × 8 patches into 16 × 16 patches, then 16 × 16 patches into 32 × 32 patches, and finally
the four 32 × 32 patches into the complete image. As in the string experiment, we normalize each conditional code by
the number of spatial locations in its parent patch, average within each join scale, and combine the three scale averages
with a power mean using p = 0.75. This gives equal overall weight to each spatial scale while placing greater pressure
on the weakest ones.
Optimization.
The image space is far too large to search directly, so we search over compact procedures that generate
images. Specifically, we use compositional pattern-producing networks (CPPNs): coordinate-based neural networks
that specify a single image by mapping each normalized spatial coordinate to its channel values (Stanley, 2007). We
can thus treat a CPPN’s topology and parameters as a genotype and the image obtained by evaluating it across the
coordinate grid as its phenotype. Its three continuous outputs are quantized to the four intensity levels in each channel.
We optimize a population of 200 such genomes for 600 generations using the NeuroEvolution of Augmenting Topologies
(NEAT) (Stanley and Miikkulainen, 2002, 2004) algorithm. NEAT applies crossover and mutations to network weights,
activation functions, nodes, and connections while maintaining species of related networks, allowing the complexity of
the image-generating process to grow during search. This resembles the Picbreeder study (Secretan et al., 2008, 2011)
(and we use very similar hyperparameters for the evolutionary algorithm), except that our compositionality reward
replaces human selection. Both the generator and the empirical coding scheme use coordinate networks; the experiment
therefore searches for spatial structure that the chosen coding scheme is capable of recognizing, rather than claiming to
cover every possible form of image structure.
Results.
We ran the evolutionary optimization for 600 generations and only 3 seeds, given the higher computational
cost of this experiment. As in our string generation experiments, we compared compositionality-optimized images to
structure-optimized ones. Figure 3b shows that both rewards increase over the course of generations before stabilizing.
We quantitatively analyze the structure of the resulting images by computing simple network summary statistics of the
CPPN genomes that produce them in Figure 3c. We find that the average number of nodes in the evolved networks, as
well as the connections between them, is comparable for the two rewards throughout the evolutionary process. The
average network depth — which measures the longest path (in connections) from an input to an output node — also
evolves similarly. The CPPNs instead seem to differ along more fine-grained metrics that suggest the compositionality
reward produces more interconnected networks. For instance, the number of distinct input-to-output paths is greater
when optimizing the compositionality reward. Features represented by hidden nodes are also reused by more than one
downstream node more often, and fan-out to a greater number of nodes. The same is true for the inputs to these hidden
units: multiple inputs are in general combined to form downstream features more often, and in greater number. Taken
together, these results suggest that our compositionality reward results in unified structures and feature recombinations
rather than independent structures, in line with our expectations.
Our generated images themselves produce further evidence along these lines; Figure 3d shows some representative
examples, and more are provided in Appendix D.2. Compositionality-optimized images reliably look more unified:
they have distinct central themes involving repeated motifs, shapes, and colour palettes (although it is difficult to
visually establish the presence of feature hierarchies in an objective way). In contrast, structure-optimized images
appear significantly more disjoint: a common trend is that they simply pack many bands of distinct horizontal and

5
Discussion
We defined compositionality as the novel shared structure revealed when parts are modeled together. Formally, it is
the information in the best model of a whole that cannot be recovered from the best models of its parts. Applying this
comparison at successive nodes of a decomposition tree yields a profile that reflects the locations and scales at which
novel compositional structure emerges. We tested this definition by using it as a generative objective. Optimizing strings
and images for prescribed profiles produced objects with recognizable nested reuse and coherent organization across
scales. Because the objective constrains the form of this organization while remaining agnostic to its particular content,
these results suggest that the definition isolates the intended part–whole organization rather than merely rewarding
complexity, regularity, or shared information.
The success of deep learning.
This view provides one explanation for why depth is a useful inductive bias. If natural
data reveals novel structure whenever smaller regions are combined into larger ones, then an effective model should
likewise build representations of larger-scale structure from those available at smaller scales. Successive layers of a
deep network provide exactly this form of capacity: later features can represent combinations and reorganizations of
earlier ones, without requiring the same composition rule at every scale. Standard architectures nevertheless impose
a fixed maximum depth and are usually trained on iid datapoints of a fixed extent. For nonstationary streams whose
structure continues to grow across temporal scales, the framework instead motivates more flexible architectures whose
effective depth or hierarchy can expand whenever larger contexts reveal additional compositional structure. Reasoning
methods (Wei et al., 2022) and looped layers (Dehghani et al., 2019) provide potential building blocks towards this goal.
Curricula and open-ended learning.
The same idea shifts emphasis from specifying a compositional model directly
to constructing data whose compression requires compositional structure. Let xa be a learner’s history and xb a new
segment of experience. A large C(xa, xb →x) means that xb does more than repeat known structure, but also does
more than contribute an unrelated fact: considered with the history, it induces a new joint account that reuses or
reorganizes earlier structure. A curriculum can therefore be scored by the local compositionality introduced at each step,
or by a functional of the resulting left-to-right compositional structure profile. Searching over orderings could then test
whether curricula with high scores produce representations that are more reusable and factorized. This interpretation is
consistent with the finding of Kumar et al. (2025) that networks evolved through human-guided, open-ended search
develop more unified and factorized representations than networks trained only to reproduce their endpoints. Our
framework gives a precise hypothesis for this difference: the evolutionary history contributes substantial compositional
structure that is absent from the endpoint alone.
This objective can also guide an agent that chooses its own experience. A policy could be rewarded for selecting xb that
maximizes an empirical approximation to C(xa, xb →x), thereby constructing a curriculum through interaction. This
is closely related to intrinsic curiosity based on compression progress (Schmidhuber, 2009, 2010) and to objectives
that seek learnable structure such as Epiplexity (Finzi et al., 2026). The difference is the constraint placed on how
the learner’s model changes: the reward favours novel structure that becomes useful through its relationship to prior
experience, rather than novelty or learnability alone. Repeated over time, this provides an operational criterion for
open-ended learning in which knowledge continues to grow by building on itself.
Future work and limitations.
The ideal quantities in our definition are uncomputable, and the empirical version
depends on restricted model classes, imperfect MDL search, and conditional codes that provide only operational
surrogates for algorithmic complexity. Our experiments show that useful approximations are possible, but not how
stable the resulting scores are across compressors or optimization procedures. Establishing this robustness, and
identifying which approximation errors preserve comparisons between objects, are important next steps.
A second, crucial open question that we highlighted in Section 3 is: which decompositions should be considered for
a particular object? The compositional structure profiles we defined here are relative to a decomposition tree, and
different trees can expose different structures in the same object. However, is there any sense in which an object
admits an “intrinsic” decomposition, and if so how can it be defined? A systematic account should determine which
decompositions expose useful, robust structure and which merely introduce artifacts. We conjectured that the tree which
maximizes the sum of compositionality scores is a particularly good candidate for a canonical decomposition, but
this must be further investigated. Ultimately, these questions reflect a broader feature of compositionality: any claim
about how a whole is composed must identify its constituent parts. Traditional accounts often fix these constituents

Appendix A
Practical approximation of compositional structure
The definitions in the main text are uncomputable, since they are rooted in algorithmic information theory. In practice,
however, they can be approximated using empirical compressors, model classes, learning algorithms, and minimum
description length procedures.
Estimating compositionality empirically involves three distinct difficulties:
1. K(m) is uncomputable, yet is required to define the Occam’s razor model mx in Equation (5).
2. K(mx | mx1, . . . , mxk), which defines the amount of novel compositional structure in Definition 1, is likewise
uncomputable.
3. Identifying mx requires searching over the potentially infinite model class M and determining that no
unexamined model yields a shorter two-part description.
The third difficulty is distinct from evaluating the complexity of any particular candidate model. Kolmogorov complexity
is upper semicomputable: by running candidate programs in parallel and retaining the shortest description found so far,
one obtains successively tighter upper bounds that eventually reach the optimal value, although there is no general way
to recognize when this has occurred. Bédard and Bergeron (2022) make the analogous observation for Kolmogorov’s
structure function. This gives the theoretical search an anytime interpretation: continued search can reveal better models
and shorter descriptions, but it does not provide a computable certificate that the current model is optimal. A practical
MDL procedure replaces this open-ended search with a specified model class, optimization method, and finite search
budget.
We replace the uncomputable quantities with empirical code lengths induced by practical coding schemes. Let f denote
an empirical two-part coding scheme used to fit models by minimum description length, together with its associated
model representation and search procedure. Let g denote an empirical conditional coding scheme used to measure
the additional cost of describing the model of the whole given the models of the parts. These coding schemes may be
specialized to the particular data modality, model class, and training procedure under consideration.

Theorem 1 (Compositionality implies algorithmic mutual information). Adopt the notation from Section 3. Suppose

(23)

Intuitively, the theorem states that novel structure appearing only when the parts are modeled jointly generally makes
the whole more compressible than the parts considered separately, and therefore implies positive algorithmic mutual
information. The no-tie assumption excludes the exceptional case in which changing to a novel model of the whole
reduces the model-description cost but increases the residual code length by exactly the same amount, leaving the total
two-part description length unchanged. This is a mild assumption: changing the model will ordinarily change the total
two-part compression cost, whereas an exact cancellation between its model and residual components requires a precise
tie. The formal proof of the theorem follows.
Note 2. Technical status of the mutual information result
The above theorem and proof below use the paper-wide convention of suppressing additive coding terms and are
therefore an idealized description-length argument, not a literal theorem about prefix Kolmogorov complexity
without further qualifications. With full bookkeeping, combining independent descriptions, identifying an
optimal two-part code with K(x), and writing K(mx | mxa, mxb) = 0 each introduce machine-dependent
overheads; the relevant bounds may also contain logarithmic terms when lengths or boundaries must be encoded.

K(xa, xb) assumes that the decomposition boundary is available as side information. Consequently, the strict
tests C > 0 and I > 0 can be altered by the suppressed terms. A fully rigorous variant would replace zero
by significance thresholds exceeding the relevant overheads and strengthen the no-tie assumption to require a
compression margin exceeding them. The idealized result is retained because it isolates the intended qualitative
relationship: novel joint structure ordinarily yields a compression advantage, whereas mutual information can
also arise merely from reusing structure already present in the parts.

MDL grammar induction.
Standard RePair maintains the current residual sequence z, finds its most frequent
adjacent pair of symbols, introduces a new rule for that pair, and replaces the pair’s non-overlapping occurrences by the
new rule name (Larsson and Moffat, 2000). It repeats this process until no pair occurs twice. For example, replacing
01 by A changes 01010 to AA0 and adds the rule A →01. Since the symbols of z can include previously introduced
rule names, later replacements can build a hierarchy of rules. Two features of standard RePair are undesirable for
our purpose. First, it discovers a long repeated substring through several pair replacements, even when one rule for
the entire substring would be shorter and easier to interpret. MR-RePair addresses this problem by starting from a
repeated pair and extending all of its occurrences to the left and right for as long as their surrounding symbols agree,
then introducing a rule for the resulting longer repeat (Furuya et al., 2018). Second, RePair and MR-RePair ordinarily
add a rule whenever a suitable repetition exists. They do not ask whether the saved occurrences outweigh the cost of
storing the rule, and can therefore assign substantial grammars even to random strings for which the empty grammar
gives a shorter two-part code.
Our fitter retains MR-RePair’s long-repeat search but chooses among its candidates by the resulting change in Lf
G(xv);

appends a symbol only if the same symbol appears immediately after every occurrence. This continues until neither
extension is possible. The result is maximal relative to this set of occurrences; it need not be the longest repeat
anywhere in z. As in MR-RePair, if the result has length greater than two and its first and last symbols are equal, we
remove its final symbol. This boundary adjustment prevents shifted, self-overlapping copies from inducing inconsistent

The pair-extension procedure has an important blind spot. It extends a pair only while all occurrences of that pair have
the same neighbouring symbols. Suppose, for example, that several copies of a long pattern contain the pair 01, but 01
also occurs elsewhere in unrelated contexts. Those extra occurrences can stop the simultaneous extension immediately,
even though the subset inside the long pattern would yield a valuable rule. We therefore supplement the local pair
search with a global repeat search based on a suffix array. This second search can isolate a subset of starting positions
that share a long pattern.
A substring is any contiguous segment of a sequence. A prefix is a substring that begins at the first position, while
the suffix beginning at position i is the substring zi, zi+1, . . . that runs from i to the end. The suffix array does not
store these suffixes themselves: it stores their starting positions, ordered so that the corresponding suffixes are in
lexicographic, or dictionary, order. For example, the suffixes of z = cabdab, shown in sorted order, are

We summarize this shared-prefix information with a longest-common-prefix (LCP) array. For each suffix after the
first in sorted order, its LCP value is the number of initial symbols it shares with the preceding suffix. A block of r
consecutive suffixes for which the r −1 intervening LCP values are all at least h represents r occurrences of the same
length-h substring. We enumerate the maximal such blocks at the prefix lengths present in the LCP array; these are the
LCP intervals in Algorithm 2. An interval is only a quick proposal, since its occurrences may overlap and replacing
overlapping copies is impossible. We rank an interval containing r suffixes with shared-prefix length h by h(r −1),
which approximates the number of repeated symbols beyond the first copy. This ranking is used only to decide which
proposals receive further evaluation, not to accept a grammar rule.
For each retained interval, we truncate its shared prefix to the maximum permitted rule length and use that prefix as
the candidate pattern q. We then search the entire residual for every exact occurrence of q, rather than using only
the starting positions in the interval. Scanning from left to right, we keep an occurrence whenever it begins after the
preceding retained occurrence ends; because all occurrences have equal length, this produces a maximum-cardinality
non-overlapping set. Patterns with fewer than two retained occurrences are discarded, and duplicate patterns proposed
by different intervals are evaluated only once. Every surviving suffix-array pattern is finally scored with the same exact
MDL gain ∆(q) as every pair-derived pattern.

rather than imposing a hard cap on its output. The routine can return as many as B distinct patterns when all retained
intervals survive, and every returned pattern is evaluated exactly before a rule is accepted.

Conditional model compression.
At a binary join xv = [xa, xb], the conditional compressor receives the grammar
G fitted to the combined substring and the two grammars Ga, Gb fitted independently to its parts. It does not receive
their residual sequences: the target quantity is the cost of describing the model mxv given the part models, not the cost
of reconstructing xv. Operationally, the compressor tries to describe each rule of G as either a copy of, a modification
of, or an addition to the rules already available in Ga and Gb. A copied rule should cost zero, a modified rule should
cost according to the changed symbols, and a genuinely new rule should be charged in full.

The first problem is determining which rules correspond. Rule names are arbitrary identifiers: for example, the two
grammars (A →01, B →AA) and (C →01, D →CC) have the same structure even though their names differ.
Directly comparing their serialized symbol sequences would incorrectly charge for this relabelling. We therefore seek a
consistent relabelling of each part grammar, meaning that we both reorder its rule definitions and replace every reference
to an old rule name by the corresponding new name. Only after this alignment do we compare rule contents.
To handle grammars with different numbers and lengths of rules, we place them in arrays with a common number of
rows and columns and fill unused rows and positions with a distinguished padding symbol. When this common number
of rule rows is at most 6, we can try every possible relabelling of a part grammar and retain the one with the smallest
symbolic difference from G. This exhaustive procedure compares the actual rules and therefore finds the minimum-cost
relabelling under the difference measure defined below.
For larger grammars, trying every relabelling is infeasible because the number of permutations grows factorially. We
instead give each rule a fixed-length numerical descriptor, or embedding, constructed from its terminal symbols, their
positions, and the rules that it references. These features depend on the rule’s role in the grammar rather than its integer
name. Rules with similar descriptors can then be treated as candidate counterparts.
Mechanistically, each rule is represented by a 64-dimensional vector. Every terminal symbol is assigned a fixed
random vector generated with seed 0 and shared across all three grammars. At initialization, a reference to another
rule contributes no content beyond its position, so a rule’s descriptor summarizes its terminals and overall layout. We
then refine all rule descriptors simultaneously for three rounds. In each round, every rule reference is replaced by the
preceding-round descriptor of the referenced rule before the enclosing rule is summarized again. One round therefore
incorporates the contents of directly referenced rules, and successive rounds incorporate progressively deeper nested
structure. This procedure is analogous to Weisfeiler–Lehman refinement (Leman and Weisfeiler, 1968), and a consistent
relabelling of rule names leaves the resulting descriptors unchanged.
Because a rule’s right-hand side is an ordered sequence, we add a sinusoidal position vector to the vector at each symbol
position. Before averaging the position vectors into one descriptor for the rule, we replace the vector uk at position
k by 0.25uk−1 + 0.5uk + 0.25uk+1, repeating the endpoint vector when a neighbour lies beyond the rule boundary.
This local smoothing makes the descriptor less sensitive to a one-position displacement caused by a small insertion or
deletion. We compute the squared Euclidean distance between every rule descriptor in G and every descriptor in a part
grammar, then use the Hungarian assignment algorithm to find the one-to-one correspondence with minimum total
descriptor distance. This is only a tractable heuristic for choosing a relabelling: the final conditional score is computed
from the aligned symbolic rules, not from their embedding distances.

Optimization.
We implemented the evolutionary search with pymoo (Blank and Deb, 2020), using NSGA-II (Deb
et al., 2002) with a single objective equal to the negative reward. Although NSGA-II is formulated for multi-objective
optimization, in this setting its tournament selection and parent–offspring survival reduce to an elitist genetic search
over a scalar score; we refer readers to the original work for details of ranking and survival.
The initial population contains 10,000 strings sampled independently and uniformly from the eight-symbol vocabulary.
Each generation produces 5,000 offspring. Selected parent pairs undergo two-point crossover with probability 0.9.
Mutation is then applied to every offspring: each of its 128 positions is independently selected with probability 2/128,
and a selected position is reset to a uniformly sampled vocabulary symbol, possibly the symbol already present. Thus,
each offspring has two attempted symbol resets in expectation. Duplicate strings are eliminated. Parents and offspring
are scored, and NSGA-II retains an elite population of 10,000 strings for the next generation.

We evolve these genomes with feed-forward NEAT (Stanley and Miikkulainen, 2002, 2004), implemented using
