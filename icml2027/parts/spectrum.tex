The same terms can sort the accounts of compositionality that the field already uses, and the sorting explains why so many current claims concern weak compositionality alone. We place twenty-two accounts on one spectrum, ordered by the capabilities each asks for (Figure~\ref{fig:spectrum}). Each account meets five conditions about decomposition, listed in Section~\ref{sec:sets}, and its place follows from the answers (Table~\ref{tab:spectrum}).


\subsection{Five sets of capabilities}
\label{app:sets}

A \emph{blend} composes parts and keeps no decomposition in roles, so no procedure returns each part in its role. \emph{Weak compositionality} asks the system to act correctly on new combinations of training parts. \emph{Strong compositionality} further asks a linear probe, fixed in advance, to return every part in every role for parts new to it (Definition~\ref{def:decomp}). \emph{Shared role codes} further require each part to enter every whole through one vector that changes only with its role (Definition~\ref{def:factor}), which lets a written vector or an edit act on one role alone. \emph{Explicit copies} further require that vector to be the part itself, copied into the whole \citep{fodor1988}. The states of each set support every capability of the sets before it, although a system exhibits those capabilities only when its own procedures use its states. Strong compositionality fixes the kind of procedure that decomposes, shared role codes fix its form, and explicit copies fix the vector. In our tests, explicit copies add a form without adding a capability, because the concatenation reference already reaches $\kappa = 1$.

\textbf{Placing accounts.} Frege's principle and Montague's homomorphism fix how a whole's meaning is built and ask nothing of the way back, so they sit in the weak set. Reverse compositionality adds the way back \citep{fodor2002}, and functional compositionality asks for general procedures that build and decompose every whole \citep{vangelder1990}. Both accounts ask for strong compositionality, and our fixed probe supplies the generality they leave open. Vector-symbolic architectures sit in two places. Clean-up against a stored vocabulary decomposes in the weak way, whereas a probe recovers new parts only while the width holds every part. Tensor products, disentangled codes and additive decoders fix one code per role. Compression and Kolmogorov measures ask for shared role codes and test them in the direction from parts to whole on training data \citep{andreas2019,elmoznino2024,elmoznino2026}.

\textbf{Placing a new account.} Five conditions, checked in order, place any account. (a) An account that scores no decomposition of the whole into parts sits in the weak set when it scores outputs on new combinations, and in the blend set when it composes parts without marking their roles. (b) An account whose decomposer sees only training parts is weak, because a lookup passes. (c) An account with no probe class fixed in advance lies outside the spectrum, because every injective system passes, and an account with a fixed class is strong. (d) An account whose role codes share one vector per part across roles, with residual $\delta \approx 0$, asks for shared role codes. (e) An account whose whole tokens each constituent explicitly, so that the vector is the part itself, asks for explicit copies. An account can meet different conditions for parts and for roles, which gives split verdicts such as ``Weak in parts, blend in roles'' in Table~\ref{tab:spectrum}.

\textbf{Hupkes et al.'s five tests.} \citet{hupkes2020} divide compositionality into five tests, and the sets place most of them. Systematicity recombines known parts, including known parts in new roles, so the weak set covers it, and the further demand that a part new to the system fill a role it never filled belongs to the capability of that name, which needs shared role codes. Productivity asks for wholes larger than any in training, which our nesting capability tests in the strong set. Substitutivity asks that a synonym leave the output unchanged, and our converse-paraphrase test asks the same of a role. Localism asks whether a system computes a whole from the states of its parts alone, a property of the procedure that stays behavioral and unplaced here. Overgeneralisation asks whether a model applies a rule to its exceptions, a behavioral test outside the spectrum.

\textbf{Tests outside the spectrum.} Two common tests sit outside the spectrum, because every injective system passes them. Unrestricted probes and recodings fix no kind of probe, while probes scored on training parts test no part new to the probe.

\subsection{Modern tests and methods}
\label{app:modern}

Four families of tests in current machine learning score the weak form, and a fifth family, mechanistic tests, can reach the strong form (Table~\ref{tab:modern}). Composable diffusion is a composition method, and we list it beside the tests because its symmetric sum marks no roles. Each behavioral test scores the outputs of a system's own procedure on new combinations of known parts, and each inherits two blind spots: a lookup over the training vocabulary passes it (Lemma~\ref{lem:lookup}), and a failure cannot say whether the system lost a part or merely failed to read it. We take the families in turn.

\textbf{Benchmarks of compositional generalization.} SCAN, COGS, CFQ and SLOG train a model on some combinations of primitives and test it on others \citep{lake2018,kim2020cogs,keysers2020,li2023slog}. Their test parts are training parts, so the benchmarks score weak compositionality by design. This holds even for tests that place a known primitive in a context it never filled, such as the add-primitive split of SCAN or the new roles of COGS, because a known part in a new role is a new combination of training parts. The capability of Table~\ref{tab:capabilities} asks more, namely that a part new to the system enter a role it never filled without training. These benchmarks also score only the model's output. Our glyph networks show the cost of that choice. Their decoders fail on new glyphs, whereas a new decoder trained on the same frozen states renders them, so a benchmark of outputs would misreport the contents of the state.

\textbf{Vision--language benchmarks.} Winoground and ARO pair an image with a caption and with the same caption with its roles swapped \citep{thrush2022winoground,yuksekgonul2023}. Image--text encoders score near chance, and \citet{yuksekgonul2023} conclude that these models behave like bags of words. Our image encoders reproduce the failure, at 0.50--0.51, yet their patch states keep each photograph in a fixed place with $\kappa = 0.81$--$0.94$, and 0.18--0.49 for three of them when the pair moves across the canvas. The bag of words describes the pooled embedding that matching reads, while the patch states inside the encoder still hold the places, mostly as absolute positions. Other work locates the loss elsewhere or questions the benchmarks themselves. Text encoders bottleneck compositionality in contrastive models \citep{kamath2023textenc}, models struggle with spatial relations \citep{kamath2023whatsup}, CLIP binds concepts poorly when two objects compete \citep{lewis2024clip}, and SugarCrepe shows that ARO-style negatives fall to text-only cues \citep{hsieh2023sugarcrepe}.

\textbf{Text-to-image evaluation.} T2I-CompBench and GenEval generate images from captions that bind attributes, counts and places, and they score the images with detectors or question answering \citep{huang2023t2i,ghosh2023geneval}. Generated scenes misplace objects and attach attributes to the wrong object \citep{conwell2022,rassin2022}. These scores describe the end of a long chain that runs from a text encoder through a denoiser to a detector, and a failure anywhere along it lowers them. Section~\ref{sec:diffusion} follows the chain stage by stage in SD 1.5: generated layouts ignore the relation, the caption state follows word order, the denoiser keeps places only as fixed quadrants of real photographs and loses them once the photographs move (Section~\ref{sec:images}), and on its own generated images it barely names the nouns.

\textbf{Composition of diffusion models.} Composable diffusion combines concepts by adding the score of each concept \citep{liu2022composable}, and synthetic studies of concept space track the order in which diffusion models learn to combine attributes \citep{okawa2023,park2024hidden} and the rules of a random hierarchy \citep{favero2025diffusion}. A sum of scores is symmetric in its concepts, so the composition itself marks no roles, as $x + y$ marks none for two vectors. The sum can place both concepts in a scene, and any role must then come from elsewhere, such as the wording of each prompt or the layout of the image. In SD 1.5, swapping the two concepts leaves the image unchanged, as a check of the symmetry, and place words in each prompt leave the figure in its place only at chance for every relation tried, at 0.42--0.53, with 0.51 for left and 0.53 and 0.42 for top and bottom, and guidance weights from 3 to 12 leave left/right at 0.36--0.48 (Section~\ref{sec:diffusion}). Energy terms that bind each concept to a relation, as in \citet{liu2021relations}, give a counterpart that marks roles. \citet{park2024hidden} also find that models acquire a combination of concepts well before prompting reveals it, a gap between hidden ability and behavior that our hypothesis predicts.

\textbf{Compositional reasoning in language models.} Multi-step tasks score whether a model composes facts or operations that it handles alone \citep{dziri2023,press2023}. \citet{press2023} find that models answer both single-hop questions and still fail their composition at a rate that stays roughly constant as the models grow. This compositionality gap concerns the procedure that composes, and our results suggest asking first whether the intermediate state holds both parts. Probing and patching work on the bridge entity takes a step toward the strong version \citep{yang2024latent,biran2024hopping}. Where language models answer role questions by meaning, the state they answer from carries the asked role, built once the question arrives, and patches of that state move the answer (Section~\ref{sec:behavior}).

\textbf{Mechanistic tests of structure.} Structural compositionality looks for subnetworks that implement each subroutine \citep{lepori2023}, while interchange interventions and tensor-product decompositions look for part vectors inside states \citep{geiger2021,geiger2024das,mccoy2019}. These methods inspect the system itself, so they can reach the strong form, and \citet{feng2024} test role binding directly through binding IDs. They seldom score their maps on parts new to the map, however, and an unrestricted map passes every injective system (Lemma~\ref{lem:psi}). Each becomes a test of strong compositionality once it fixes the kind of map in advance and scores it on new parts.

\textbf{Modern systems bind roles to position.} Across the pretrained systems we tested, the parts that states return sit in slots set by position: the order of mention in sentence states (Section~\ref{sec:origins}) and the absolute location in image and denoiser states (Section~\ref{sec:images}), while generation, a behavior, takes its layout from the noise seed and the nouns (Section~\ref{sec:diffusion}). Such states pass a probe test on new parts under a positional part map, yet they fail the converse paraphrase and lose most of their recoverability in the moved scene, two tests that Definition~\ref{def:decomp} requires because roles follow meaning. A role code by meaning appears only at the question in language models that answer by meaning, and mostly for the asked role (Section~\ref{sec:behavior}). Under positional roles these states reach the strong form, and under roles by meaning they fall short of it, so we place each as a pair: strong by position, short of strong by meaning (Table~\ref{tab:tested}). \citet{feng2024} find that language models bind entities to attributes through binding IDs in context, which is a positional mechanism of the same kind.

\subsection{A claim should name the capabilities it tests}
\label{app:claims}

We propose that a claim of compositionality name the set of capabilities it tests and state the terms of that test. A claim of weak compositionality scores the system's own procedure on new combinations. A claim of strong compositionality states its probe, part vectors and new parts, and a claim of shared role codes also reports the role residual $\delta$.
